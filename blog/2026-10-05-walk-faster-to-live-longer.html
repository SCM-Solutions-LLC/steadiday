Walk Faster to Live Longer After 50

October 05, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

You lace up your shoes after breakfast, head out the door, and figure you're doing something good for yourself. You probably are. A large new study suggests that how fast you walk may matter alongside how far you walk, though, like most research of its kind, it shows a link rather than proof of cause and effect. The point is to understand what drives the benefits of walking, especially for walking pace and longevity over 50, rather than to chase an arbitrary step goal.

The Study That Changes the 10,000-Step Conversation

Published on September 8, 2026, in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, this large wearable-device study from the University of Sydney used data on 64,743 UK Biobank adults (average age 62). Each person wore a wrist activity tracker for one week, and the researchers then followed them for about eight years, during which 1,697 died. They looked at the combination of how many steps people took and how quickly they took them. Because the study was observational, it shows links, not proof that walking faster makes you live longer.

Pace was measured as each person's average steps per minute during their 30 busiest walking minutes of the day, which did not have to be in a row. Walking 7,500 to 10,000 steps a day at a peak pace of around 100 steps per minute was linked to the lowest rates of death from any cause and from cardiovascular disease. The finding that caught the most attention involved people who took fewer than 5,000 steps a day. Compared with people at that step count who walked very slowly (about 45 steps per minute), those who walked faster had a substantially lower risk of dying, similar to the risk of people who took 5,000 to 7,500 steps at a slower pace. As Harvard Health highlighted on October 2, 2026, the authors say a brisk pace of at least 90 to 100 steps per minute for 30 minutes a day is a promising target for longevity, and that flexible combinations of steps and speed can be good options for people with limited time or ability.

Step count still matters. A 2025 meta-analysis in Lancet Public Health that reviewed 57 studies found that 7,000 steps per day was associated with a 47% lower risk of death from any cause compared with 2,000 steps per day, based on pooled data from 14 of those studies. A separate 2022 analysis of 15 studies and about 47,000 adults, also in Lancet Public Health, found that for adults 60 and older the drop in risk leveled off at about 6,000 to 8,000 steps a day. So you don't have to grind toward 12,000 steps if your joints or schedule won't cooperate. Pace may do some of that work for you.

What 100 Steps Per Minute Feels Like

A hundred steps per minute sounds clinical, but in practice it's fairly intuitive. A 2023 review in the journal GeroScience notes that researchers have estimated moderate-intensity walking at about 100 steps per minute or more. It's a pace where you're moving with purpose: faster than window-shopping, slower than power-walking in a race. You're breathing a little harder, and you could hold a conversation but wouldn't want to give a speech.

A useful real-world test: try walking to the beat of a song around 100 BPM. "Walking on Sunshine" by Katrina and the Waves runs about 109 BPM. "Can't Stop the Feeling" by Justin Timberlake is close to 113. You don't need to match the beat exactly; the point is to train your body to recognize what "brisk" feels like compared with a casual amble.

Most people underestimate how slowly they're walking when they're not paying attention. On a relaxed morning walk, it's easy to drift into a slower pace without realizing it. That's still good movement, but in this study, a faster pace for part of the day was linked with lower risk.

Morning: Set Your Pace Before the Day Runs Away

Morning is the easiest time to build a brisk walk into your day, mostly because there are fewer competing demands. You haven't yet fielded emails, started errands, or talked yourself out of it. If you can get outside within an hour of waking up, even for 20 minutes, you're locking in your most important health habit before decision fatigue sets in.

A simple structure works well. Spend the first five minutes walking at whatever pace feels natural. This is your warm-up, and it matters, especially if your joints need a few minutes to loosen up. Then gradually pick up the pace until you're in that brisk zone, with purposeful, slightly elevated breathing. Hold that for 15 to 20 minutes, then ease back off for a few minutes at the end. You don't need a track, a coach, or a heart rate monitor to do this.

Pick up speed only where the ground is smooth and even. If you feel unsteady on your feet or have fallen before, the CDC's fall prevention advice is to ask your doctor to check your risk of falling first. Stop walking and get medical help right away if you have chest pain or tightness, pain in your neck, jaw or arms, dizziness or faintness, severe shortness of breath or a heartbeat that isn't regular, as Mayo Clinic's guide to exercise and blood pressure advises. Chest pain can be one of the signs of a heart attack, and the CDC says to call 911 immediately.

Midday: Short Bursts Count More Than You Think

Life doesn't always cooperate with a single dedicated walk. If your morning got away from you, or your knees were grumpy, the middle of the day offers real opportunities to accumulate brisk movement in shorter windows. The federal Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans no longer require activity to come in bouts of at least 10 minutes to count, because the total amount of moderate-to-vigorous activity is what is linked to health benefits.

A 10-minute brisk loop around the block after lunch, followed by another after your afternoon coffee, adds up faster than it seems. You're looking for chunks of time where you're moving with intention, rather than 10,000 steps in one go. The key word is brisk, meaning engaged rather than hurried or stressful.

This is also a good time to rethink how you move between activities. Parking a little farther away, taking stairs instead of elevators, and walking while on a phone call all count. When you're already working toward a daily cadence goal, these moments contribute.

Afternoon: When Your Body Is Most Ready to Move

Some people find their joints feel looser and their legs feel stronger later in the day. If you have flexibility in your schedule, an afternoon walk can be a pleasant break.

This is a good time for a slightly more structured walk if you're up for it. Try picking up your pace during the middle portion (maybe a five-minute brisk push), then backing off. It's a simple form of interval walking, and it doesn't require any equipment or special training. Those brisker minutes count toward the 150 minutes a week of moderate activity that the CDC recommends for adults 65 and older.

Your SteadiDay app, connected to Apple Health, can show you how your afternoon steps compare to your morning ones over time. Patterns emerge that you'd never notice day-to-day, like realizing you consistently walk faster on Tuesdays, or that your cadence drops in the afternoon when you haven't had enough water.

Walking Pace and Longevity Over 50: The Bigger Picture

Consider what this research represents. These were wearable-device measurements from over 64,000 people, who wore trackers for a week and were followed for about eight years, rather than self-reported estimates. The message is encouraging: you don't need to be an athlete, and you don't need perfect joints, a gym membership or two free hours a day. You need to walk, and to walk with some intention behind it.

The combination of a reasonable step count (7,500 to 10,000 per day) and a brisk pace (around 100 steps per minute, at least during part of your walk) may be a realistic target for many adults over 50. If 10,000 steps isn't realistic for you right now, this study suggests that picking up the pace during your shorter walk is a reasonable approach.

Think of walking speed as a kind of volume knob for your health investment. You can turn it up when your schedule is tight or your body won't cooperate with longer distances, and that flexibility is what makes this finding so useful in daily life.

Common Questions

How fast is 100 steps per minute when walking, and how can I tell if I'm hitting that pace?

100 steps per minute works out to roughly one step every 0.6 seconds, which researchers have estimated is about where walking becomes moderate intensity. A simple way to check: count your steps for 15 seconds and multiply by four. If you hit 25 or more, you're in the right range. You can also use a free metronome app on your phone or match your stride to music around 100 BPM.

Does walking pace matter more than total steps for adults over 60?

Both matter. In the 2026 BJSM study, adults who took fewer than 5,000 steps a day at a faster pace had a lower risk of death than very slow walkers at that step count, similar to the risk of people who took 5,000 to 7,500 steps at a slower pace. The study was observational, so it shows a link, not proof that pace makes up for fewer steps. Still, if hitting 10,000 steps isn't realistic for you, picking up the pace during a shorter walk is a reasonable option.

Is it safe to walk at a brisk pace if I have joint pain or haven't exercised in a while?

For most people with mild joint pain or a sedentary background, a gradual increase in walking pace is safe, but check with your doctor first if you have heart or lung problems, high blood pressure, significant joint issues, or have fallen before or feel unsteady. Start at your comfortable pace, walk for consistency for a week or two, then gradually pick up speed during the middle portion of your walk, on smooth, even ground. Stop and get medical help right away if you have chest pain, pain in your neck, jaw or arms, dizziness or faintness, severe shortness of breath or a heartbeat that isn't regular. For chest pain, call 911.

How many days a week do I need to walk briskly to see longevity benefits?

The BJSM study measured walking during a single week and did not test how many days of brisk walking are needed. For a weekly goal, the CDC recommends that adults 65 and older get at least 150 minutes of moderate activity such as brisk walking, for example 30 minutes a day on 5 days, plus muscle-strengthening activities on at least 2 days and activities that improve balance.

Updated November 15, 2026: We corrected our description of the British Journal of Sports Medicine study: people wore trackers for one week and were followed for about eight years (not nearly a decade), pace meant their 30 busiest minutes of the day, and the study shows links, not cause and effect. We replaced a broken link to the study, attributed the 6,000 to 8,000 step finding for adults 60 and older to the 2022 analysis it came from, corrected the answer about how many days a week to walk, removed unsourced claims about afternoon performance and interval walking, and added warning signs that mean stop and get help, plus advice for people who feel unsteady.
