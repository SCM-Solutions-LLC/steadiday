Sodium and Your Heart: How Much Is Too Much?

October 15, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 6 min read
Editorially reviewed by SteadiDay Health Editorial Team

Earlier this year, the American Heart Association released modeling data with a striking projection: if packaged and prepared foods simply met existing sodium-reduction targets, the U.S. could prevent roughly 100,000 cases of ischemic heart disease and 25,000 strokes over the next 20 years. Not through surgery. Not through new drugs. Just by lowering the salt in everyday food. That number stops you cold — and it raises an uncomfortable question for most of us over 50: do you actually know how much sodium you're eating every day?

The honest answer, for most people, is no. And the research suggests that's a problem we can no longer afford to ignore when it comes to sodium intake and heart health.

What the Latest Research Is Telling Us

A landmark 2023 crossover trial published in JAMA tested 213 adults between the ages of 50 and 75 — people a lot like the SteadiDay community. Researchers had participants follow a high-sodium diet for one week, then a low-sodium diet the next. The result? Switching to a low-sodium diet reduced systolic blood pressure by an average of 8 mm Hg in just seven days. That's roughly the same reduction you'd expect from a first-line blood pressure medication.

Here's what made the finding especially significant: it held up regardless of whether participants already had hypertension and regardless of whether they were already taking blood pressure medication. This wasn't about helping sick people get less sick. It was a demonstration that the heart responds to sodium in almost everyone — and it responds fast.

Then came a 2024 report from the American College of Cardiology that added another layer of urgency. Researchers looked at more than 3,100 people already diagnosed with heart disease and found that 89% of them were exceeding the recommended 1,500 mg daily sodium limit — consuming, on average, more than twice that amount. These are people who've already had a cardiac event or diagnosis. And still, the salt keeps coming.

Why Sodium Hits Harder After 50

Sodium isn't a villain in small amounts. Your body needs it to regulate fluid balance, support nerve function, and keep muscles working. The trouble is the dose — and the way aging changes how your body handles it.

As you get older, your kidneys become less efficient at flushing out excess sodium. Blood vessels naturally stiffen with age, making them more sensitive to the fluid-volume pressure that too much salt creates. And because your thirst signals dull over time, you may not be drinking enough water to help offset a salty meal the way a younger person might. All of this means the same bag of chips, the same restaurant entrée, the same can of soup that might have rolled off your system at 35 can create real cardiovascular stress at 60 or 70.

The guidelines reflect this biological reality. The American Heart Association recommends keeping sodium intake under 2,300 mg per day for most adults — roughly one teaspoon of salt — and under 1,500 mg if you have high blood pressure, heart disease, or kidney disease. To put that in perspective: a single fast-food meal can contain 1,500 to 2,500 mg of sodium on its own. One meal.

The Hidden Sodium Problem: It's Not the Salt Shaker

Most people blame the salt shaker. That's the wrong culprit. Only about 11% of the sodium Americans consume comes from salt added at home during cooking or at the table. The other 70-plus percent comes from processed, packaged, and restaurant foods — and much of it is invisible.

Bread. Deli meat. Canned soup. Cottage cheese. Breakfast cereal. These aren't foods most people think of as "salty," but they are among the top contributors to daily sodium intake. A single cup of canned tomato soup can contain 700 to 900 mg. Two slices of deli turkey can clock in at 500 mg or more. Even foods marketed as "light" or "healthy" can be sodium landmines if you don't check the label.

This is why the AHA's food-supply modeling data matters so much. Individual willpower is one tool, but when the baseline sodium level in everyday food is high, the deck is stacked against you before you've made a single choice.

Practical Ways to Lower Your Sodium Intake Today

You don't have to overhaul your entire diet overnight. Small, specific changes compound quickly — especially given how rapidly the body responds, as the JAMA trial showed.

Read labels by serving size. The Nutrition Facts panel lists sodium per serving, and serving sizes are often smaller than you think. Check what a realistic portion looks like before you assume a food is low in sodium.

Choose "low sodium" or "no salt added" versions of pantry staples. Canned beans, tomatoes, broth, and tuna all come in reduced-sodium versions that taste nearly identical once they're incorporated into a dish.

Cook at home more often — and lean on acid and aromatics. Lemon juice, vinegar, garlic, herbs, and spices enhance flavor without sodium. When food tastes vibrant, you notice the absence of salt far less.

Treat restaurant meals as an occasional indulgence, not a baseline. Restaurant portions are notoriously high in sodium. When you do eat out, ask for sauces and dressings on the side, and skip the bread basket.

Track for a week before you judge. Most people are genuinely surprised by their actual intake once they log it. Awareness is the first step to change.

How Monitoring Your Heart Health Fits Into the Picture

Lowering your sodium intake is powerful, but it works best as part of a broader habit of paying attention to your cardiovascular health. That means checking your blood pressure regularly, staying active, and making sure someone close to you knows if something feels off.

Common Questions

How much sodium should adults over 50 consume per day for good heart health?

The American Heart Association recommends no more than 2,300 mg of sodium per day for most adults — about one teaspoon of salt. If you have high blood pressure, heart disease, or kidney disease, the target drops to 1,500 mg per day. Most Americans currently consume well above both thresholds.

How quickly can reducing sodium intake lower blood pressure?

Surprisingly fast. A 2023 JAMA crossover trial found that adults aged 50–75 who switched from a high-sodium to a low-sodium diet saw their systolic blood pressure drop by an average of 8 mm Hg in just one week. This effect occurred regardless of whether participants had hypertension or were already on blood pressure medication.

Where does most of the sodium in the American diet actually come from?

Roughly 70% or more of daily sodium comes from processed, packaged, and restaurant foods — not from salt added at home. Major contributors include bread, canned soups, deli meats, cheese, and fast food. Cutting back on these categories tends to have a bigger impact than putting away the salt shaker.

Is a low-sodium diet safe if I'm already taking blood pressure medication?

For most people, yes — and it may make your medication work more effectively. The JAMA trial specifically included people on antihypertensive medications and still found meaningful blood pressure reductions from dietary changes alone. That said, always talk to your doctor before making major dietary changes if you're on medication, especially diuretics, since sodium and fluid balance interact closely.
