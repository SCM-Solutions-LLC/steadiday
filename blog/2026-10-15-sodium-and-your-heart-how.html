Sodium After 50: Daily Limits and a Hidden-Salt Food Table

October 15, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 6 min read

Earlier this year, the American Heart Association released modeling data with a striking projection: if packaged and prepared foods met existing sodium-reduction targets, the U.S. could prevent roughly 100,000 cases of ischemic heart disease and 25,000 strokes over the next 20 years, without surgery or new drugs, by lowering the salt in everyday food. That projection raises an uncomfortable question for anyone over 50: do you know how much sodium you're eating every day?

For most people, the answer is no, and the research on sodium intake and heart health suggests that's a problem we can no longer afford to ignore.

What the Latest Research Is Telling Us

A 2023 crossover trial published in JAMA tested 213 adults between the ages of 50 and 75 (people a lot like the SteadiDay community). Each person spent one week on a high-sodium diet and one week on a low-sodium diet of about 500 mg a day, in an assigned order. Compared with the high-sodium week, switching to a low-sodium diet reduced systolic blood pressure by an average of 8 mm Hg in just seven days. The researchers called that effect comparable to a commonly used first-line blood pressure medication.

The finding was especially significant because it held up regardless of whether participants already had hypertension and regardless of whether they were already taking blood pressure medication. Rather than showing how to help sick people get less sick, it demonstrated that the heart responds to sodium in almost everyone, and that it responds fast.

Then came a 2024 report from the American College of Cardiology that added another layer of urgency. Researchers looked at more than 3,100 people already diagnosed with heart disease and found that 89% of them were exceeding the recommended 1,500 mg daily sodium limit, consuming, on average, more than twice that amount. These are people who've already had a cardiac event or diagnosis, and still the salt keeps coming.

Why Sodium Hits Harder After 50

Sodium isn't a villain in small amounts. Your body needs it to regulate fluid balance, support nerve function, and keep muscles working. The trouble is the dose, and the way aging changes how your body handles it.

As you get older, your kidneys become less efficient at flushing out excess sodium. Blood vessels naturally stiffen with age, making them more sensitive to the fluid-volume pressure that too much salt creates. And because your thirst signals dull over time, you may not be drinking enough water to help offset a salty meal the way a younger person might. All of this means the same bag of chips, the same restaurant entrée, the same can of soup that might have rolled off your system at 35 can create real cardiovascular stress at 60 or 70.

Two numbers matter. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans set the upper limit for adults at less than 2,300 mg a day, about 1 teaspoon of table salt, according to the FDA, and the 2025 to 2030 edition kept that limit. The American Heart Association calls 1,500 mg a day ideal, and ACC and AHA guidelines set 1,500 mg as the limit for people with cardiovascular disease. For perspective, one restaurant serving of meat lasagna listed 2,070 mg of sodium on its label in a USDA and CDC study of popular foods, close to a full day's limit on one plate.

A one-week plan to cut back without bland meals

Each day adds one habit. Keep the earlier ones going as you add the next. The tips come from the FDA's advice on reducing sodium.

  1. Day 1: Read three labels. Pick three foods you eat every day and find the sodium and %DV per serving. Check how many servings you eat. Anything at 20% DV or more per serving is high.
  2. Day 2: Swap one pantry staple. Replace one canned food or broth with a version labeled "low sodium" (140 mg or less per serving) or "no salt added." "No salt added" means none was added during processing, so check the label, since the food may still contain some sodium.
  3. Day 3: Rinse canned foods. Rinse canned beans, vegetables and tuna in a colander before using them. The FDA says this removes some of the sodium.
  4. Day 4: Season with flavor instead of salt. Cook one meal with lemon juice or vinegar, garlic, onion, herbs, spices or a no-salt seasoning blend. Take the salt shaker off the table.
  5. Day 5: Rethink lunch meat. Make one lunch with fresh chicken, turkey or fish instead of deli meat. On fresh meat and poultry, check the package for added salt water or saline.
  6. Day 6: Cut back on condiments. Dress a salad with oil and vinegar instead of bottled dressing, choose a light or reduced-sodium condiment, and use only part of a flavoring packet.
  7. Day 7: Plan a lower-sodium meal out. Ask for your food to be made without added salt, get sauces and dressings on the side, and split an entrée or take half home. Ask if nutrition information is available. Then look back at your Day 1 labels and pick the next food to swap.

The Hidden Sodium Problem: Beyond the Salt Shaker

Most people blame the salt shaker, but it's the wrong culprit. In a study of 450 U.S. adults published in Circulation, salt added at home during cooking or at the table supplied only about 11% of sodium. About 70% was added to food before it reached the home, in processed, packaged, and restaurant foods, and much of it is invisible.

Bread, deli meat sandwiches, soup, cheese (including cottage cheese) and even packaged chicken aren't foods most people think of as "salty," but the CDC lists them among the top sources of sodium for adults. One brand of canned chicken broth listed 860 mg in a serving of about 1 cup, and a 2-ounce serving of packaged deli ham listed 500 mg (see the table above). Even foods marketed as "light" or "healthy" can be sodium landmines if you don't check the label.

This is why the AHA's food-supply modeling data matters so much. Individual willpower is one tool, but when the baseline sodium level in everyday food is high, the deck is stacked against you before you've made a single choice.

Practical Ways to Lower Your Sodium Intake Today

You don't have to overhaul your entire diet overnight. Small, specific changes compound quickly, especially given how rapidly the body responds, as the JAMA trial showed.

Read labels by serving size. The Nutrition Facts panel lists sodium per serving, and serving sizes are often smaller than you think. Check what a realistic portion looks like before you assume a food is low in sodium.

Choose "low sodium" or "no salt added" versions of pantry staples. Canned beans, tomatoes, broth, and tuna all come in reduced-sodium versions that taste nearly identical once they're incorporated into a dish.

Cook at home more often, and lean on acid and aromatics. Lemon juice, vinegar, garlic, herbs, and spices enhance flavor without sodium. When food tastes vibrant, you notice the absence of salt far less.

Treat restaurant meals as an occasional indulgence, not a baseline. Restaurant portions are notoriously high in sodium. When you do eat out, ask for sauces and dressings on the side, and skip the bread basket.

Track for a week before you judge. Most people are surprised by their actual intake once they log it. Awareness is the first step to change.

How Monitoring Your Heart Health Fits Into the Picture

Lowering your sodium intake is powerful, but it works best as part of a broader habit of paying attention to your cardiovascular health. That means checking your blood pressure regularly, staying active, and making sure someone close to you knows if something feels off.

Common Questions

How much sodium should adults over 50 consume per day for good heart health?

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans set the limit for adults at less than 2,300 mg of sodium per day, about one teaspoon of table salt. The American Heart Association calls 1,500 mg per day ideal, and ACC and AHA guidelines set 1,500 mg as the limit for people with heart disease. Americans eat about 3,400 mg a day on average, well above both numbers.

How quickly can reducing sodium intake lower blood pressure?

Surprisingly fast. A 2023 JAMA crossover trial found that adults aged 50–75 had systolic blood pressure about 8 mm Hg lower after a week on a low-sodium diet than after a week on a high-sodium diet. This effect occurred regardless of whether participants had hypertension or were already on blood pressure medication.

Where does most of the sodium in the American diet come from?

Roughly 70% or more of daily sodium comes from processed, packaged, and restaurant foods rather than from salt added at home. Major contributors include deli meat sandwiches, soups, pizza, bread, and cheese. Cutting back on these categories tends to have a bigger impact than putting away the salt shaker.

Is a low-sodium diet safe if I'm already taking blood pressure medication?

For most people, yes, and it may make your medication work more effectively. The JAMA trial specifically included people on antihypertensive medications and still found meaningful blood pressure reductions from dietary changes alone. That said, always talk to your doctor before making major dietary changes if you're on medication, especially diuretics, since sodium and fluid balance interact closely.
