RSV Vaccine After 50: Eligibility Checklist by Age (2026)

May 18, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 6 min read

In June 2025, the CDC moved the RSV vaccine age line down by ten years for adults with certain health conditions. The agency now recommends a single dose of RSV vaccine for all adults 75 and older, and for adults 50 to 74 who are at increased risk of severe RSV illness. That second part is the change worth paying attention to. If you're in your 50s or early 60s and you have diabetes that needs insulin or has caused complications, chronic lung disease, heart disease, or kidney failure, this guidance now applies to you, even if your doctor hasn't brought it up yet. This post starts with a quick eligibility checklist, then covers what changed, what RSV does to older adults, and the practical question of whether you should ask about a shot before fall.

RSV Vaccine Eligibility Checklist (2026)

Check every line that fits you, then bring this list to your pharmacy or doctor's office. It follows the CDC's adult RSV vaccine guidance for health care providers, dated February 24, 2026.

Age 75 or older

  • I am 75 or older. The CDC recommends one RSV vaccine dose for everyone in this age group, whether or not you have a health condition.

Age 50 to 74: check any that apply

The CDC recommends one dose if you are 50 to 74 and have at least one of these conditions that raise the risk of severe RSV illness:

  • Chronic heart disease, such as heart failure or coronary artery disease (high blood pressure on its own does not count)
  • Chronic lung disease, such as COPD, emphysema, asthma, interstitial lung disease or cystic fibrosis
  • Kidney failure (end-stage kidney disease) or treatment with dialysis
  • Diabetes that has caused complications, such as kidney disease, nerve damage or eye damage, or diabetes treated with insulin or an SGLT2 inhibitor
  • A nerve or muscle condition that makes it hard to clear your airway or weakens your breathing muscles, such as trouble swallowing after a stroke, ALS or muscular dystrophy
  • Chronic liver disease, such as cirrhosis
  • A chronic blood disorder, such as sickle cell disease or thalassemia
  • Severe obesity (a body mass index of 40 or higher)
  • A moderately or severely weakened immune system
  • Living in a nursing home
  • Another condition or risk factor your doctor or pharmacist thinks raises your risk, such as frailty, or living in a remote or rural area where reaching hospital care is hard

Under 50

  • I am under 50. The CDC adult recommendation does not cover you yet. The FDA has approved two vaccines, Abrysvo and mResvia, for adults 18 to 49 at increased risk, and the CDC says it is still reviewing the evidence for younger adults. If you have a serious chronic condition, ask your doctor.

Before you book

  • I have already had an RSV vaccine, in any year. You are done for now. The CDC says the RSV vaccine is not a yearly shot, and if you have already had one you should not get another at this time.
  • I feel sick today. The CDC says to wait until you recover from a moderate or severe illness. A minor illness, such as a cold, is not a reason to wait.
  • I have had a severe allergic reaction to an ingredient in an RSV vaccine. The CDC says you should not get one. Tell the pharmacist before the shot.

Questions to ask your pharmacist

  • "Which RSV vaccine do you have today?" The CDC has no preference among Arexvy, mResvia and Abrysvo, so whichever one is in stock is fine.
  • "Will my plan cover it here at no cost?" Medicare Part D plans make CDC-recommended adult vaccines, including RSV, available at no cost. Ask the pharmacist to check your specific plan before the shot.
  • "Can I get my flu shot or other vaccines at the same visit?" The CDC says RSV vaccines can be given with other adult vaccines at the same visit, though a sore arm, fever or headache may be more likely.
  • "Do you need a note from my doctor about my condition?" The CDC tells vaccinators that your own word about a risk condition is enough and that they should not turn you away for lack of medical records.
  • "Can you give me a record with the date and the vaccine name?" You will want it later, since this is a one-time shot.

What your answers mean: if you checked the age 75 box, or at least one box in the 50 to 74 list, and you have not had an RSV vaccine before, the CDC recommends one dose for you. You can get it any time of year, but late summer or early fall (August to October in most of the continental U.S.) gives the most benefit. If none of the age groups above fit you, the current CDC recommendation does not include you.

This checklist is a quick guide. It is not a substitute for advice from your doctor or pharmacist, who know your full health history.

What Changed in the RSV Vaccine Guidance, and When

When RSV vaccines first arrived in 2023, the CDC said adults 60 and older could get a dose after talking it over with their doctor, an approach called shared clinical decision-making that left a lot of judgment calls to individual doctors. In June 2024, the CDC's vaccine advisers updated that advice: all adults 75 and older, and adults 60 to 74 at increased risk, should get one dose. Then, effective June 25, 2025, the risk-based recommendation was extended down to adults 50 to 59. For adults 75 and up, the recommendation is universal: every person in that age bracket should get one dose. For adults aged 50 to 74, the recommendation kicks in when you have one or more conditions that raise your RSV risk.

That second group is where most of the practical questions come up. The CDC's list of qualifying risk factors is specific, and the checklist above walks through each one. It includes chronic lung disease (such as COPD, emphysema or asthma), chronic heart disease (such as heart failure or coronary artery disease, though high blood pressure alone does not count; see our rundown of the new 2026 heart guidelines for more on where the thresholds moved), diabetes that has caused complications such as kidney disease (relevant if you're already following developments like finerenone for chronic kidney disease) or that is treated with insulin or an SGLT2 inhibitor, kidney failure or dialysis, chronic liver disease, a moderately or severely weakened immune system, severe obesity, and living in a nursing home. Your doctor or pharmacist can also count other risk factors, such as frailty.

If any of those describe you, the current rules recommend the vaccine outright, rather than leaving it as an optional "talk to your doctor about whether you might want this" decision.

Why RSV Is a Bigger Deal Than Most People Realize

Most adults under 60 think of RSV, if they think of it at all, as a kids' illness: the virus that causes coughs and ear infections in toddlers. That framing is dangerously outdated for older adults. A three-season New York surveillance study found RSV hospitalized 137 to 256 of every 100,000 adults 65 and older each season, more than ten times the rate in adults 18 to 49, and 34 to 58 per 100,000 adults aged 50 to 64.

The hospitalization rate climbs sharply with age. CDC surveillance data shows roughly 67 RSV hospitalizations per 100,000 adults aged 50 to 64, compared to 178 per 100,000 for adults 65 and older. Among hospitalized older adults, RSV produces outcomes that look broadly similar to influenza. That's not a comforting comparison, because flu kills tens of thousands of older Americans every year.

There's also a quieter complication that's drawn more research attention recently. A study published in PMC found that cardiovascular events occurred in 18.5% of patients hospitalized for RSV. Heart attacks, strokes, new arrhythmias, and worsening heart failure all showed up at meaningfully higher rates in the weeks following an RSV hospitalization. If you already have a heart condition, RSV is a cardiovascular stressor as well as a respiratory illness.

Which Vaccine, and How Often You Need One

Three RSV vaccines are FDA-licensed and recommended for adults 50 and older: GSK's Arexvy, Pfizer's Abrysvo, and Moderna's mResvia. The CDC explicitly states that there is no preference among them, so get whichever your pharmacy or doctor has in stock. All three are single-dose, all three have been studied in large clinical trials, and all three produce strong protection against severe RSV outcomes.

The most useful detail to know: this is not an annual vaccine. One dose provides protection for at least two years, and the CDC is still learning how long it lasts. If you have already had an RSV shot, in any year, the CDC says not to get another one at this time. The CDC is currently studying whether revaccination will ever be needed and, if so, on what schedule.

Cost should not be a barrier for most older adults. Medicare Part D plans make RSV vaccines available at no cost, and most private insurance plans cover them without a copay when you use an in-network provider. Pharmacy chains and primary care offices generally stock at least one of the three options.

The Timing Question Nobody Asks About

One piece of practical advice gets far too little attention: when you get the RSV vaccine matters almost as much as whether. RSV is seasonal in the United States, with most cases occurring between late fall and early spring. Protection from the vaccine ramps up over a couple of weeks after the shot. The CDC's recommendation is to aim for August through October in most of the continental U.S., so antibody levels are high heading into the worst of the season.

That makes mid-to-late summer the best time to plan for. May, right now, is when to start thinking about it and to schedule. Don't wait until you're already coughing and wondering whether what you have is RSV, the flu, or a cold. The vaccine helps before exposure, not once you're sick.

If you're traveling internationally during the summer, ask your doctor about timing earlier. RSV seasons run differently in the Southern Hemisphere and in tropical regions, and what feels like the off-season here may be peak transmission somewhere else.

Side Effects, Honestly

The most common reactions to RSV vaccines are the same as most adult shots: sore arm, fatigue for a day or two, sometimes a mild headache or muscle ache. Serious side effects are rare. The most-discussed concern has been a small possible signal for Guillain-Barré syndrome (a rare neurological condition) in some post-marketing surveillance data, particularly for Abrysvo and Arexvy. The CDC and its vaccine advisers reviewed that signal and concluded that the benefits of RSV vaccination outweigh the potential risk for GBS in the recommended age groups. The FDA estimated that risk at roughly 10 extra cases per million vaccinated adults 60 and older.

If you have a personal history of Guillain-Barré syndrome, mention it to your doctor before vaccination. For most people, the practical risk-benefit calculation is straightforward: a sore arm versus a meaningfully lower chance of being hospitalized with a respiratory infection that could also tip a heart condition into crisis.

What to Do in May 2026

If you're 50 to 74 with any of the qualifying conditions (such as diabetes with complications, COPD, heart failure, kidney failure or dialysis, or a weakened immune system), bring up the RSV vaccine at your next medical appointment. If you don't have an appointment soon, it's worth calling your primary care office or pharmacist directly to ask. Plenty of pharmacies administer RSV vaccines without requiring a doctor's visit, especially for adults 75 and over where the recommendation is universal.

If you're 75 or older and haven't been vaccinated, the conversation is even simpler: this is a recommended preventive shot for everyone in your age group. The vaccine is at your pharmacy. Walk in or call to schedule.

One more practical step: write the vaccination date down somewhere you'll find it later. Because RSV vaccines aren't annual, two or three years from now you'll want to remember when you got yours, and "sometime around 2026" doesn't help your future doctor. RSV Vaccination in 2026 at a Glance

The CDC no longer treats RSV protection as something to negotiate case by case. It is now a recommended part of adult preventive care for two clear groups: everyone 75 and up, and adults 50 to 74 with conditions that raise their risk. It takes one dose, there are three vaccine options with no preferred brand, protection lasts at least two years, the best timing is late summer or early fall, and most adults pay nothing.

If you fit either group, the next step is a phone call to your doctor, your pharmacist, or your insurance plan to confirm coverage. Five minutes of effort now means one less thing to worry about when the first cold front of the season rolls through.

Common Questions

Who should get an RSV vaccine in 2026?

Every adult 75 and older should get one dose. Adults 50 to 74 should get one if they have a condition that raises their RSV risk, such as diabetes with complications or treated with insulin, COPD, heart failure, kidney failure or dialysis, or a weakened immune system.

How often do you need an RSV vaccine?

Once, for now. RSV is not an annual vaccine: one dose protects for at least two years. If you have already had an RSV shot, in any year, the CDC says not to get another one at this time.

Which RSV vaccine should I get?

Any of the three. Arexvy, Abrysvo and mResvia are all FDA-licensed for adults 50 and older, and the CDC has no preference among them, so take whichever your pharmacy or doctor has in stock.
