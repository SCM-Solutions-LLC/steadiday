Daytime Naps After 56: What the Science Actually Says

April 27, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

You've probably heard that a good daytime nap is practically a superpower: a Mediterranean secret to longevity, a productivity hack, a sign of a well-rested, healthy life. It's an appealing story. But a major new study published in JAMA Network Open on April 20, 2026 complicates that picture in ways worth paying attention to, especially if you're over 56. The research adds to a growing body of evidence linking certain daytime napping patterns in older adults to serious health risks, including significantly higher all-cause mortality. Below is what the science says, myth by myth.

Myth #1: Napping Is Always a Sign of Good Self-Care

This one feels true. You had a busy week, you listened to your body, you rested. That sounds healthy, but the research tells a more complicated story.

The new study, a prospective cohort study conducted by researchers at Mass General Brigham and Rush University Medical Center, tracked 1,338 adults aged 56 and older over 19 years. It stands out from earlier research in how it collected data: instead of questionnaires or self-reporting, it used wrist-worn actigraphy devices that objectively measured napping behavior around the clock, a meaningful upgrade in reliability.

The study found that longer and more frequent daytime napping was associated with significantly higher all-cause mortality risk. Each additional hour of napping per day was linked to approximately a 13% higher risk of death. That supports what a large-scale meta-analysis of 21 cohort studies, covering 371,306 participants, also found: nap durations of 30 minutes or longer were associated with increased mortality, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic disease risk.

Napping isn't inherently dangerous. But when it becomes a pattern (frequent, long, hard to control), it may be your body signaling something that deserves attention rather than encouragement.

Myth #2: A Morning Nap Is the Gentlest, Most Natural Kind

There's something cozy about a mid-morning doze in the armchair, and it seems harmless. The new JAMA study found the opposite.

Morning nappers faced a 30% higher all-cause mortality risk compared to afternoon nappers, one of the study's most surprising findings. As for why timing would matter so much, researchers suggest that napping in the morning, well before the body's natural afternoon dip in alertness around 1–3 p.m., may indicate that nighttime sleep quality is severely disrupted, or that underlying health issues are already interfering with normal sleep-wake cycles.

The human body is built for a well-timed, brief afternoon rest. An involuntary crash at 9:30 in the morning is worth a conversation with your doctor.

Myth #3: Napping More Just Means You're Getting the Rest You Need

This is the most tempting myth of all: if you're tired, sleep. But excessive daytime napping in older adults increasingly looks like a symptom rather than a solution.

There's compelling evidence linking heavy napping patterns to cognitive decline. A 14-year study using actigraphy data from 1,401 older adults in the Rush Memory and Aging Project found that longer and more frequent daytime napping was associated with up to a 1.4-fold increased risk of Alzheimer's dementia, and that the relationship runs in both directions. People with early cognitive changes nap more, and more napping may also accelerate those changes, so the cycle is worth interrupting early.

The same logic applies to cardiovascular and metabolic health. Excessive napping is often intertwined with poor nighttime sleep, sedentary behavior, depression, uncontrolled blood sugar, and cardiovascular disease, all conditions that become more common after 56. Napping more doesn't fix these issues, and it may be masking them.

Myth #4: Short Naps Are Always Safe for Older Adults

You've probably seen the headlines about the "perfect 20-minute power nap." And for many people in midlife, a short afternoon rest is fine. But the picture shifts as we get older.

The JAMA study focused on adults 56 and older, and the risk patterns it identified weren't limited to marathon afternoon sleeps. Frequency mattered as much as duration. Napping regularly, even in shorter increments, was still associated with elevated risk when the pattern was persistent and daily.

You don't need to white-knuckle your way through afternoon tiredness. But if you're consistently napping every day regardless of how well you slept the night before, that pattern is worth examining. Tracking your daily habits, including your hydration and nutrition (which both affect energy levels), can help you spot what's driving the fatigue. Myth #5: This Research Is Just Like All the Other Nap Studies

It's tempting to dismiss new health headlines as more of the same. This one is different, and the methodology is why.

Most previous nap research, including many of the studies in that 21-cohort meta-analysis, relied on self-reported napping habits. People are notoriously bad at accurately remembering how long or how often they nap, and social desirability bias can skew responses further. The 2026 JAMA study used objective, continuous wrist actigraphy measurements over nearly two decades: nineteen years of real-world data instead of survey recall.

That is a meaningful leap in scientific rigor. When researchers at Mass General Brigham (Harvard Medical School) and Rush University Medical Center say the findings are statistically significant, they have the kind of evidence that earlier nap studies couldn't provide.

What Should You Do?

This research doesn't tell you to never rest during the day. It asks you to pay attention to how and when you do.

A practical framework based on the evidence:

Keep naps short and well-timed. If you do nap, aim for 20–30 minutes in the early-to-mid afternoon. That aligns with your body's natural circadian rhythm and is least likely to interfere with nighttime sleep quality.

Look at patterns across days. One afternoon rest after a rough night is not a red flag. Napping every single day, especially in the morning, or finding it hard to get through any day without sleeping is a pattern worth discussing with your doctor.

Look upstream. Persistent daytime fatigue after 56 often has addressable causes: poor sleep hygiene, sleep apnea, low physical activity, depression, blood sugar fluctuations, dehydration, or medication side effects. These are fixable things. Logging what you eat, drink, and how you move each day can help you and your care team connect the dots faster.

Don't dismiss the cognitive angle. Given the bidirectional link between excessive napping and Alzheimer's risk, treating unexplained daytime sleepiness as a cognitive health issue as well as a tiredness issue could matter more than most people realize.

Putting It Together

The story we've been telling ourselves about daytime naps (that they're a harmless pleasure, a Mediterranean longevity secret, something to lean into without question) is incomplete. One of the most methodologically rigorous studies on the topic, tracking 1,338 older adults with objective wrist-worn devices over 19 years, found clear associations between longer, more frequent, and especially morning napping and significantly higher all-cause mortality risk in adults 56 and older.

That's a reason to pay attention, not to panic. Your afternoon energy levels, your sleep quality and your napping habits are all data points. Rather than white-knuckling through fatigue, try to understand what's causing it so you can address it. That's what living well after 56 looks like.
