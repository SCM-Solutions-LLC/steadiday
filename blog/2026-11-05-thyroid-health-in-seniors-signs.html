Thyroid Health in Seniors: Signs Your Levels May Be Off

November 05, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

The 2023 Korean Thyroid Association guidelines, reviewed in a paper in Endocrinology and Metabolism, do not recommend routine levothyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism, a mildly abnormal thyroid result that many older adults carry. The guidance leans heavily on the TRUST trial, which tested thyroid hormone in adults 65 and older and found no improvement in symptoms. For anyone focused on thyroid health in seniors, the finding changes a common assumption: a slightly off number does not automatically call for a prescription.

This post covers what the guidelines changed, which signs deserve a conversation with your doctor, and how to approach testing after 50.

What the 2023 guidelines changed

Subclinical hypothyroidism means your thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) is above the reference range while your thyroid hormone (free T4) is still normal. It is often found by accident on routine bloodwork. For years, many people with this pattern were started on levothyroxine, the standard replacement hormone, on the theory that treating early would prevent symptoms or later problems.

The 2023 Korean Thyroid Association guidelines take a more cautious position. They do not advocate routine levothyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism, citing the TRUST trial in adults 65 and older, where treatment did not improve symptoms compared with placebo. Treatment decisions are meant to be individual, taking into account how high TSH is, your symptoms, your age and your other health conditions.

Why thyroid symptoms are easy to miss after 50

Overt hypothyroidism happens when the thyroid doesn't make enough hormone, which slows metabolism. The Cleveland Clinic lists fatigue and unexpected weight gain among the most common effects, and notes it is usually treated with hormone replacement.

The difficulty is that fatigue, weight change, constipation, dry skin and low mood are also common explanations for ordinary aging, medication effects, poor sleep or depression. A review of thyroid dysfunction in older adults found that it often shows up with subtle or atypical symptoms, which is why diagnosis and treatment need age-specific consideration. In an older adult, an overactive thyroid may look like unexplained weight loss, weakness, anxiety or a fast or irregular heartbeat rather than the classic sweating and tremor described in younger patients.

Signs your levels may be off

No single symptom confirms a thyroid problem, but certain patterns are worth raising with your doctor.

Signs that can point to an underactive thyroid include:

  • Fatigue that persists despite adequate sleep
  • Weight gain you can't explain by changes in eating or activity
  • Feeling cold when others are comfortable
  • Constipation, dry skin, or thinning hair
  • Slowed thinking, low mood, or a hoarse voice

Signs that can point to an overactive thyroid include:

  • Unintended weight loss despite a normal appetite
  • A racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat
  • Restlessness, anxiety, or trouble sleeping
  • Muscle weakness or tremor
  • Feeling hot, or sweating more than usual

An irregular heartbeat, chest pain or severe shortness of breath needs prompt medical attention whatever the cause.

What testing involves

The usual first step is a TSH blood test. If it is abnormal, your doctor typically adds free T4 and sometimes thyroid antibodies, which can show whether an autoimmune condition such as Hashimoto's disease is behind the change. A single borderline TSH is often repeated after a couple of months, since the number can drift back to normal on its own.

Several things can complicate the picture. Recent illness, certain medications, and biotin supplements (common in hair and nail products) can all affect results. Tell your doctor about everything you take before the test, and ask whether a supplement should be paused beforehand.

Treating a borderline result

If your TSH is mildly elevated and your free T4 is normal, the TRUST trial evidence suggests that starting levothyroxine is unlikely to make you feel better if you are 65 or older. That does not mean the result should be ignored. Your doctor may recheck it, look for thyroid antibodies, and consider treatment if TSH is markedly high or symptoms are significant. Be aware that the guidelines address subclinical disease specifically. People with overt hypothyroidism, where thyroid hormone is actually low, are still generally treated with replacement.

The review on older adults also stresses that dosing matters at this stage of life. Taking too much levothyroxine can produce symptoms of an overactive thyroid, including heart rhythm problems, so doses are usually adjusted carefully and monitored with follow-up blood tests.

How to prepare for the appointment

Because symptoms overlap with so many other conditions, a record helps your doctor sort them out. For two weeks, jot down your energy level, sleep, weight, bowel habits and heart rate if you check it. Bring a complete list of medications and supplements, and mention any family history of thyroid disease. Ask these questions:

  • Is my TSH high, low or borderline, and was free T4 checked?
  • Should we repeat the test, and when?
  • Could a medication or supplement be affecting the result?
  • If I'm 65 or older with a mild elevation, what are the pros and cons of treating?

Common Questions

What are the first signs of thyroid problems in older adults?

Often they are subtle: lingering fatigue, unexplained weight change, constipation, feeling unusually cold or hot, or changes in mood and thinking. In older adults, symptoms can be atypical or mild, so a blood test is the only reliable way to tell. Raise persistent changes with your doctor rather than assuming they are age-related.

Do I need medication if my TSH is slightly high?

Not necessarily. The 2023 Korean Thyroid Association guidelines do not advocate routine levothyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism, citing the TRUST trial in adults 65 and older, which showed no symptom improvement. Your doctor may repeat the test and weigh your symptoms, age and other conditions before deciding.

How often should adults over 50 get their thyroid checked?

There is no single schedule that fits everyone. Many doctors check TSH when symptoms appear, when starting certain medications, or as part of routine bloodwork if you have risk factors such as autoimmune disease or a family history. Ask your doctor what interval makes sense for you.

Can supplements affect thyroid test results?

Yes. Biotin, found in many hair, skin and nail supplements, can interfere with some thyroid lab tests. Tell your doctor about every supplement you take and ask whether to pause any before your blood draw.
