Vitamin B12 Deficiency and Fracture Risk After 50

October 08, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read
Editorially reviewed by SteadiDay Health Editorial Team

The conversation about bone health after 50 has long centered on calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise. But a large new study published in Scientific Reports in October 2026 suggests there's another nutrient that belongs in that conversation — one that millions of older adults are quietly running low on. Researchers analyzing nearly 1.9 million health records found that adults aged 50 and older with vitamin B12 deficiency faced a 33% higher fracture hazard and a 50% higher rate of repeated falls compared to people with normal B12 levels. Those are not small numbers. And for most people, B12 doesn't even come up at their annual physical.

What the Study Actually Found

The research, "Vitamin B12 Status and Long-Term Fracture Risk in a Multicenter Propensity Score–Matched Cohort," drew on data from the TriNetX Global Collaborative Network — a massive, real-world dataset spanning dozens of health systems. Researchers compared 115,735 adults aged 50 and older who had been diagnosed with B12 deficiency against more than 1.76 million adults with normal B12 levels.

The fracture numbers tell a clear story: 7.2% of people with B12 deficiency experienced a fracture, compared to 6% in the normal group. After statistically adjusting for other health factors, that gap translated to a 33% higher fracture hazard — rising to 37% higher during the first five years of follow-up. Beyond broken bones, the deficient group showed a 22% higher rate of osteoporosis and, strikingly, a 50% higher rate of repeated falls.

One important caveat, and the researchers said this plainly: this is an association, not proof of causation. B12 deficiency doesn't necessarily cause fractures, and having low B12 doesn't mean a fracture is coming. But an association this consistent, across this many people, is worth taking seriously.

Why Vitamin B12 Deficiency Fracture Risk Over 50 Is a Real Concern

So why would B12 — a nutrient most people associate with energy levels — have anything to do with bones and balance? There are actually several plausible biological pathways, and researchers outlined them carefully.

First, B12 is essential for cell division and proper neurological function. When levels drop, nerve conduction slows. That matters because the nerves controlling your muscles, your balance, and your ability to catch yourself when you stumble are all part of the same system. Peripheral neuropathy, muscle weakness, and an unstable gait are all documented consequences of B12 deficiency — and all of them raise fall risk.

Second, B12 and folate are critical cofactors in homocysteine metabolism. When B12 is low, homocysteine levels rise. And elevated homocysteine is increasingly recognized as a problem for bone quality — specifically, it appears to interfere with collagen cross-linking, which is part of what gives bone its structural integrity. A retrospective cohort study in middle-aged and older adults found that elevated homocysteine was associated with a 33% higher hazard of developing osteoporosis per standard deviation increase in homocysteine levels. That's a separate line of evidence pointing at the same underlying mechanism.

Third, separate research has looked at the musculoskeletal picture more broadly. A study examining serum vitamin B12 and its biomarkers in adults over 65 found that higher B12 levels were positively associated with lean muscle mass, while elevated homocysteine was linked to reduced gait speed, lower knee extensor strength, and lower bone mineral density. Weaker muscles, slower movement, less bone density — that combination is exactly what sets the stage for a fall that ends in a fracture.

Why B12 Levels Drop After 50

Here's the part that surprises a lot of people: you can eat plenty of B12 and still end up deficient. That's because B12 absorption depends on a protein called intrinsic factor, produced in the stomach. As you age, stomach acid production naturally declines — and without adequate acid, the process that releases B12 from food and binds it to intrinsic factor for absorption starts to break down. It's a gradual process, which is why deficiency often develops slowly and without obvious symptoms until it's been going on for a while.

Certain medications accelerate the problem. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) — the acid-reducers commonly prescribed for reflux and heartburn — further suppress stomach acid. Metformin, widely used for type 2 diabetes, is also associated with reduced B12 absorption. Given that both of these medications are common among adults over 50, the overlap with B12 deficiency is significant.

Vegetarians and vegans face additional risk, since B12 is found almost exclusively in animal products. And people who've had gastric bypass surgery may have structural changes that limit absorption regardless of diet.

What You Can Do About It Today

The most actionable takeaway from this research is simple: ask your doctor to check your B12 level. It's a standard blood test, it's inexpensive, and it's often not included in routine panels unless you specifically request it. If you take a PPI or metformin, or if you eat little to no meat or dairy, that's worth mentioning when you ask.

If your levels come back low, supplementation is straightforward — and because supplements deliver B12 in a form that doesn't depend on intrinsic factor for absorption (it's absorbed directly through the gut wall in high doses), they work even when the stomach's natural absorption system is impaired. Your doctor can recommend the right form and dose for your situation.

On the bone side, it's worth making sure the rest of your bone-health picture is solid: adequate calcium and vitamin D, regular weight-bearing activity, and a conversation with your provider about whether a bone density scan (DEXA) makes sense for you. These aren't either/or choices — B12 is one piece of a broader picture.

The Bigger Picture on Routine Screening

What makes this study particularly noteworthy isn't just the size of the effect — it's the size of the dataset. Nearly 1.9 million health records is an unusually large foundation for this kind of analysis. That scale helps filter out the noise that can skew smaller studies, and it gives researchers more confidence that the patterns they're seeing are real.

The authors stopped short of recommending universal B12 screening, noting that more research — ideally randomized controlled trials — is needed to determine whether correcting B12 deficiency actually reduces fracture risk. That's a fair scientific caution. But from a practical standpoint, a blood test with no real downside, that can reveal a treatable deficiency, seems like a reasonable step for anyone in their 50s or beyond who hasn't had one recently.

Bone health conversations tend to happen after something goes wrong — after a fracture, after a DEXA scan flags low density. This research is a nudge to have those conversations earlier, and to make sure B12 is part of them.

Common Questions

How do I know if I have a vitamin B12 deficiency that might affect my fracture risk?

The only way to know for certain is a blood test. Many people with low B12 have no obvious symptoms for years, which is why it's often missed. Ask your doctor to include B12 in your next routine blood work, especially if you take metformin or acid-reducing medications, eat little or no meat, or are over 60.

Can taking a B12 supplement actually lower my risk of fractures or falls?

That question hasn't been definitively answered yet. The October 2026 study found an association between B12 deficiency and higher fracture and fall rates, but it wasn't designed to test whether supplementation reverses that risk. What is clear is that correcting a deficiency restores normal neurological function and homocysteine metabolism — both of which matter for balance and bone quality. Talk to your doctor about whether supplementation makes sense for you.

What foods are highest in vitamin B12, and should I be eating more of them?

B12 is found almost exclusively in animal products — clams and beef liver are among the richest sources, followed by fish, meat, eggs, and dairy. However, if your stomach acid production has declined with age (very common after 60), you may not absorb B12 from food efficiently regardless of how much you eat. That's why a blood test, not a dietary adjustment alone, is the right starting point.

If I've already been diagnosed with osteoporosis, should I specifically ask about B12?

Yes, it's a reasonable question to raise. The research links B12 deficiency to both lower bone density and a higher rate of repeated falls — two factors that directly affect fracture risk in people already managing osteoporosis. A simple blood test can tell you whether your levels are in the normal range, and if they're not, supplementation is safe and well-tolerated for most people.
