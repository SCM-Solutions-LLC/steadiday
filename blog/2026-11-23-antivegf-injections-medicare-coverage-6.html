Anti-VEGF Injections Medicare Coverage: 6 Steps to Take

November 23, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

The American Academy of Ophthalmology's Weekly Journal Update of November 18, 2026 featured a new, editor-selected paper in Ophthalmology. It compares how often people on Medicare Advantage and people on traditional Medicare receive anti-VEGF eye injections, which treat wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD), diabetic eye disease and similar conditions. The timing matters, because fall open enrollment is when many adults 65 and older choose plans. This post covers what anti-VEGF injections Medicare coverage can mean for your treatment, and six steps you can take today.

The summary of the study was brief, and exact figures should come from the full paper, so this post does not report its results or say that one plan type is better. What it can do is show you where coverage rules touch your care and what to check before you commit to a plan.

Why Coverage Rules Matter for Injections

Anti-VEGF drugs block a protein that causes leaky, abnormal blood vessels in the retina. Treatment often means an injection into the eye every month or two, sometimes for years. Missing visits can allow vision to worsen, so anything that delays an appointment, such as paperwork or a drug swap, deserves attention.

Medicare Advantage plans can use tools that traditional Medicare does not, including prior authorization and step therapy (trying one drug before another). A retrospective review of wet AMD eyes presented at ASRS 2025 found that 47% of eyes in Medicare Advantage plans with step therapy were switched to a different anti-VEGF drug within a year, compared with 31% under traditional Medicare, which does not use step therapy. That is a separate study from the Ophthalmology analysis, but it shows how plan rules can shape which drug you receive.

Step 1: Ask Your Retina Office Which Drug You Get and Why

Call the office and ask for the name of your current anti-VEGF drug and whether your insurer required you to try another one first. Write down both answers. The reason: if a plan uses step therapy, a switch can happen for coverage reasons, and knowing this lets you ask your doctor whether the change fits your eye. The switching figures above come from the ASRS 2025 review.

Step 2: Look Up Prior Authorization Before Enrollment Closes

Open the plan's formulary and "Evidence of Coverage" document, then search for the words "prior authorization," "step therapy" and "Part B drugs." Anti-VEGF drugs are typically given in a doctor's office, so they usually fall under Part B rather than Part D. Do this before the enrollment deadline, because after it closes your options are limited. Medicare's Plan Finder and your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) can help you read these documents; SHIP counseling is free.

Step 3: Confirm Your Retina Specialist Is in Network

Ask the office, then confirm with the plan by phone, whether your retina specialist and the injection site are in network for next year. Ask for the answer in writing or note the date, time and name of the representative. Traditional Medicare generally lets you see any provider that accepts Medicare, while Advantage plans use networks, so a specialist you trust could be out of network after a plan change.

Step 4: Add Up Your Likely Yearly Cost

Count how many injections you had this year, then multiply by what the plan charges per visit. Include the drug, the office visit and any imaging. Under traditional Medicare, Part B usually covers 80% of the approved amount after the deductible, and many people carry a Medigap or other supplement for the rest. Advantage plans set their own copays and out-of-pocket maximums. Find the maximum on the plan summary, since a person getting injections every month may reach it. Ask the retina office's billing staff for the typical charges they see.

Step 5: Put Injection Dates and Transportation on One Calendar

Treatment schedules can be tight, and a delayed authorization can push back a visit. Put each appointment on a paper or phone calendar the day you book it, and note the date the authorization expires. Arrange a driver now, because your vision may be blurry after dilation. Call the office a week ahead to confirm the approval is on file. If a visit is delayed, ask the office whether it can be rescheduled sooner rather than waiting.

Step 6: Check Each Eye Every Day for Warning Signs

The American Academy of Ophthalmology's AMD Preferred Practice Pattern advises monitoring each eye's near vision, for example with an Amsler grid, so you notice early symptoms such as metamorphopsia (wavy or distorted lines) or decreased vision that suggest wet AMD and need prompt evaluation. Tape an Amsler grid to the refrigerator or bathroom mirror. Each morning, cover one eye, look at the center dot from reading distance with your usual glasses, and note any wavy lines, gaps or a dark spot in the center. Then switch eyes.

Call your eye doctor the same day if you notice these changes, and seek emergency care for sudden vision loss, a curtain over your vision, or new flashes and floaters. After an injection, call promptly for increasing eye pain, worsening redness or falling vision. Do not wait for your next scheduled visit.

Keeping Care Steady Whichever Plan You Pick

Once the full Ophthalmology paper is in hand, you and your doctor can weigh its figures against your own situation. Until then, the steps above work with either plan type. For related reading, see our posts on vision problems and sleep in older adults and preventive care this fall.

Common Questions

Does traditional Medicare use step therapy for anti-VEGF injections?

No. The ASRS 2025 review describes traditional Medicare as not using step therapy, while some Medicare Advantage plans do. Check your specific plan's rules each year, since they can change.

Are anti-VEGF eye injections covered under Medicare Part B or Part D?

They are usually given in a doctor's office, so they typically fall under Part B. Advantage plans must cover Part B services but can add rules such as prior authorization. Confirm details with your plan.

What does wet macular degeneration look like at first?

Early signs include straight lines that look wavy, blurred or missing areas near the center of vision, or a dark spot. Checking each eye separately, such as with an Amsler grid, can reveal changes. Call your eye doctor promptly if you notice any.

Can I change Medicare plans if my injections are delayed?

Options depend on timing. Open enrollment runs October 15 to December 7, and other windows apply in certain cases. A SHIP counselor can explain which choices you have.
