How to Build a Bedtime Routine That Actually Works

June 18, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

If you've been in bed for eight hours most nights and still wake up exhausted, it's easy to put it down to age. Before you do, walk through what you do in the hour before bed. Scrolling through a tablet until your eyes burn, a glass of wine to "wind down," the TV murmuring in the background while you drift off: none of it feels dramatic, but all of it can quietly wreck your rest. Before reaching for a prescription, it's worth building a bedtime routine that works for your body and your life.

Why Sleep Changes After 50 and Why a Bedtime Routine You Can Stick To Matters

The sleep problems that creep in after 50 aren't imaginary, and they aren't inevitable either. Your body's internal clock does shift with age: you naturally start feeling sleepy earlier and wake earlier than you did at 35. Your production of melatonin, the hormone that signals nighttime to your brain, decreases. You also spend less time in each stage of sleep, including deep sleep. The result is sleep that feels lighter, more fragmented, and less satisfying, even when you're technically logging enough hours.

The NIH's National Institute on Aging identifies insomnia as the most common sleep problem in adults over 60 and recommends a bedtime routine built around relaxing activities, such as reading, soothing music or a warm bath, along with going to bed and getting up at the same time every day. In other words, what you do in the hour before bed is worth as much attention as how long you stay in bed.

The Science Behind the Routine

Sleep habits can be tested. A 2025 peer-reviewed study published in Frontiers in Sleep randomly assigned 119 adults aged 50 to 79 to watch a short sleep hygiene video, watch the video and get nightly text reminders for four weeks, or do neither. Both education groups reported better sleep quality and less daytime sleepiness than at the start, without medication, while the control group did not. The fitness trackers participants wore showed no change in measured sleep, and the study was small and short, so treat it as encouraging rather than proof. Your habits affect your sleep one way or another; the question is whether they're working for you or against you.

Building Your Wind-Down Hour

Harvard Health's January 2025 guide on sleep hygiene is specific about timing: reserve a full hour before bed, not fifteen minutes, to step away from anything stimulating. For most people, that's the hardest part, because the evening is often the only quiet time you get, and it's tempting to fill it with news, social media, or one more episode of whatever you're watching.

That hour has a biological purpose: your nervous system needs a real transition from the alertness of the day to the calm that sleep requires. Dimming the lights in your home for that last hour is one of the simplest steps you can take. Bright light, especially the blue-wavelength light from phones and tablets, suppresses melatonin production and signals "daytime" to your brain even at 10 p.m. Swapping overhead lights for lamps, or using a warm-toned bulb, may help you feel sleepy sooner.

Replace screen time with something that calms your particular nervous system. For some people that's reading a physical book. For others it's light stretching or gentle yoga, a warm bath, which both Harvard Health and the National Institute on Aging suggest, or listening to music or a podcast at low volume. The specific activity matters less than the consistency. Your brain is pattern-hungry: when you do the same sequence of calming things every night, it begins to interpret those actions as a signal that sleep is coming, and it starts preparing accordingly.

The Details That Derail You

Caffeine lasts longer than most people realize, and it is in tea, chocolate and soda as well as coffee. Harvard Health suggests avoiding caffeine after lunch if it keeps you awake at night, so if you're waking in the small hours, your afternoon latte may deserve more scrutiny than your mattress. The same goes for alcohol. A glass of wine can make you sleepy, but Harvard Health notes that after a few hours alcohol becomes stimulating, can wake you and make it hard to fall back asleep, and reduces REM sleep. The National Institute on Aging advises avoiding alcohol in the evening, even small amounts.

Your sleep environment is doing quiet work, too. Most people sleep better in a slightly cool room, and Harvard Health suggests keeping it around 65 to 68 degrees Fahrenheit at night. Darkness matters too, since even small amounts of light from a streetlamp, a charging device, or a digital clock can disrupt melatonin levels. Blackout curtains or a sleep mask are low-tech, low-cost fixes. And if your partner's snoring or early-morning sounds are waking you, don't underestimate earplugs or a white noise machine. There's no virtue in sleeping through disruption when the fix can be that simple.

Consistency Is the Whole Game

What trips most people up is the weekend. You stay up two hours later on Friday, sleep in Saturday, and suddenly your body clock has jetlag without any plane ride. Sleep researchers call this "social jetlag." The National Institute on Aging advises going to sleep and getting up at the same time each day, even on weekends or when traveling. A steady wake time in particular helps anchor your body clock. Over time, your body learns when to feel tired and when to feel awake, and sleep starts to come more naturally.

If you've built a good routine and still lie awake most nights, snore loudly, or feel sleepy during the day, talk with your doctor. Ongoing insomnia and sleep apnea are treatable. Check with your doctor or pharmacist before trying sleep medicines or supplements such as melatonin too. The National Institute on Aging notes that sleep medicines may help in the short term but carry risks, including dependence and feeling groggy the next day, and should not be used long term.

Starting Small

You can start with a few changes. Move your tablet charging station out of the bedroom. Switch from wine to herbal tea around 8 p.m. Spend 20 minutes reading by a dim lamp before bed: same chair, same lamp, same time, every night. You don't need to overhaul your life to build a bedtime routine your body can use. It does require deciding that your sleep is worth protecting, and then protecting it, one consistent evening at a time.

Common Questions

What should a healthy bedtime routine for seniors include?

A good bedtime routine for adults 50 and older typically involves dimming lights and putting screens away for about an hour before bed, a calming activity like reading or gentle stretching, and going to sleep and waking up at consistent times each day. Avoiding caffeine after lunch and keeping your bedroom cool and dark round out the basics. The specific activities matter less than doing the same sequence consistently, because your brain learns the pattern and responds to it.

How long does it take for a new bedtime routine to start working?

There's no fixed timeline. In the 2025 Frontiers in Sleep study, adults 50 and older reported better sleep after four weeks of sleep hygiene education. Consistency matters, including on weekends, so give a new routine about a month before drawing conclusions. If you still sleep poorly after that, talk with your doctor.

Is it normal to need less sleep after age 60?

Somewhat, but less than most people think. Older adults need about the same amount of sleep as other adults, seven to nine hours a night, according to the NIH's National Institute on Aging. What does change is how you sleep: you tend to go to bed and wake up earlier, and you spend less time in each sleep stage, including deep sleep. Feeling like you need less sleep is often a sign of poor sleep quality rather than a reduced need for sleep.

Can a bedtime routine help with waking up in the middle of the night?

It can, particularly when combined with limiting alcohol and caffeine. Harvard Health notes that alcohol can wake you a few hours after you fall asleep, and caffeine late in the day can keep you awake. A calming routine and a steady schedule can make sleep more settled. If middle-of-the-night waking is persistent, it's worth discussing with your doctor, as it can also be linked to sleep apnea or other treatable conditions.
