Brain Training With Boosters Linked to 25% Less Dementia

April 06, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

About 10 hours of speed training spread over five or six weeks, followed by booster sessions roughly one and three years later, was linked to a 25% lower risk of diagnosed dementia over the next two decades. That is what a 20-year follow-up of the ACTIVE cognitive training trial, published in February 2026 by researchers at Johns Hopkins, the University of Pennsylvania and other universities, found. The benefit came from one specific type of cognitive training, not from the memory or reasoning courses tested alongside it. The boosters mattered: people who did the first five weeks without them showed no meaningful drop in risk.

The Johns Hopkins Discovery

The ACTIVE trial enrolled 2,802 adults aged 65 and older in 1998 and 1999 and randomly assigned them to memory training, reasoning training, speed training, or no training. For this follow-up, researchers linked the records of 2,021 participants to their Medicare claims from 1999 to 2019, giving about 20 years of data. Only one approach was linked to lower risk: cognitive speed training. Published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: Translational Research & Clinical Interventions, the study found that adults who completed up to ten 60- to 75-minute sessions over five to six weeks and then had booster sessions at 11 and 35 months had a 25% lower risk of a dementia diagnosis over 20 years than people who had no training. By the end, about 40% of that group (105 of 264) had a dementia diagnosis, compared with about 49% of the untrained group (239 of 491). Speed training without the boosters made no significant difference.

The finding stands out for how long it lasted. The authors point out that no earlier cognitive training program had been shown to lower dementia risk over a 20-year period. Participants were 65 to 94 when they enrolled, with an average age of about 74, so the follow-up tracked many of them well into their 80s and 90s. The training focused on improving how quickly people could take in and respond to visual information, especially when their attention was split.

Understanding Cognitive Speed Training

Unlike traditional brain games that focus on memory or problem-solving, cognitive speed training targets your brain's processing speed: how quickly you can take in information, make sense of it, and respond appropriately. Think of it as upgrading your brain's operating system to run faster and more efficiently.

In ACTIVE, the speed training ran on a computer and focused on visual search and processing increasingly complex information in shorter and shorter glimpses, with an emphasis on divided attention: taking in more than one thing at once. The task was adaptive, so it got harder as each person improved, pushing the brain to process information at increasingly rapid speeds.

The ACTIVE researchers did not scan participants' brains, so exactly how speed training might lower dementia risk is still an open question. The authors suggest the type of task matters: speeded, dual-attention, adaptive exercises set this training apart from the memory and reasoning courses, which were not linked to lower risk.

The SuperAgers Connection

The ACTIVE findings sit alongside recent research on "SuperAgers," people aged 80 and older whose memory scores match or beat those of people in their 50s. An NIH-funded study of hippocampal neurogenesis published in Nature in February 2026 examined donated brain tissue and found that SuperAgers had a distinct pattern of new neuron growth in the hippocampus, the brain's memory center, which the authors describe as a possible "resilience signature." SuperAgers had about twice as many immature neurons as healthy older adults, though with only six SuperAger brains in the study, that difference was not statistically significant.

The two studies looked at very different things, and neither shows that speed training creates new neurons or turns anyone into a SuperAger. Together they point the same hopeful way: some brains stay resilient well into the 80s, and a specific kind of practice in later life was linked to a later dementia diagnosis.

Practical Cognitive Speed Training You Can Start Today

The study used a computer task, so none of these is the exact protocol, but they work on the same idea, and you can start any of them today:

The Flash Card Challenge: Create cards with simple shapes, colors, or numbers. Flash each card for 2-3 seconds, then immediately cover it. Try to identify what you saw as quickly as possible. As you improve, reduce the flash time to 1 second or less.

Center and Edge: Ask someone to hold up a card with a small picture in the middle and a dot near one edge for a second or two, then lower it. Name the picture and point to where the dot was. As you improve, have them show it more briefly and move the dot farther out. This is the closest of these to the study's task, which trained divided attention: taking in more than one piece of visual information in a brief glimpse.

Speed Sorting: Set a timer for 30 seconds and sort a deck of cards by suit as quickly as possible. Track your progress and try to beat your previous score each time. This trains your brain to categorize information rapidly while maintaining accuracy.

Visual Search Tasks: Hide specific objects in complex images or scenes, then challenge yourself to find them as quickly as possible. Start with obvious targets and progress to more subtle ones. Many smartphone apps offer this type of training.

Building Your 5-Week Training Program

In the study, participants did up to 10 sessions of 60 to 75 minutes over five to six weeks. Those who finished at least eight of them could be assigned booster training about 11 and 35 months later, with up to four 75-minute sessions each time. A home version of the first block could look like this:

Week 1-2: Focus on basic speed exercises for 20-30 minutes, 4 times per week. Start with longer exposure times (2-3 seconds) and gradually reduce them as you improve.

Week 3-4: Introduce more complex exercises with multiple elements to track simultaneously. Maintain your 4 sessions per week but increase intensity rather than duration.

Week 5: Challenge yourself with the most difficult variations you can handle while still maintaining reasonable accuracy (aim for 70-80% correct responses).

Year 1 and year 3: Repeat a short block of up to four sessions about 11 months after you finish and again at about 35 months. In the study, people who skipped these boosters showed no meaningful drop in dementia risk.

Remember to stay consistent with your training schedule. In the study, the five-week block on its own was not enough; the lower risk showed up only when the boosters followed. As with physical exercise, sporadic training won't deliver the same benefits as a structured, consistent program.

Staying Safe While Training Your Brain

While you train, keep in mind that overall safety and independence are crucial for long-term brain health. Apps like SteadiDay offer support for adults 50+, including Fall Detection features that automatically alert emergency contacts if a fall is detected. Physical safety and cognitive health go hand in hand, since falls can significantly impact brain health through injury or reduced confidence. Having these safety measures in place lets you focus on your brain training with peace of mind.

Cognitive speed training can be done anywhere, anytime. Whether you're at home, traveling, or waiting for an appointment, you can engage in exercises that are actively building your cognitive reserves. This accessibility makes it easier to maintain the consistency needed to achieve the long-term benefits linked to speed training in the research.

Start your 5-week cognitive speed training program today. Your future self, 20 years from now, will thank you for taking this step toward lifelong brain health. The window to build cognitive resilience is open now, and a few weeks of training, with refreshers later, could change the trajectory of your cognitive future.
