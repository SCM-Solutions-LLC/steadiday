Advance Directives: 5 Things People Most Often Get Wrong

May 07, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

Nobody hands you a guidebook for this. A sudden stroke can leave someone in the ICU, unable to speak or decide, while their family struggles because nobody knew what they wanted. That is the situation advance directives planning exists to prevent. It can feel like a morbid chore, but it is one of the most loving things you can do for the people you care about most. These are five things that often go wrong, and how to avoid them.

1. A Living Will Can't Anticipate the Moment, but Your Proxy Can

You might assume that writing down your wishes in a living will is enough. It feels thorough and finished. But a living will is a static document written in advance, and real medical emergencies are rarely that tidy.

No living will can cover every possible scenario: a sudden cardiac event, a traumatic brain injury, an unexpected complication after routine surgery. That's why naming a health care proxy may be the more powerful move. The NIH National Institute on Aging explains that a health care proxy (someone legally designated through a durable power of attorney for health care) can make real-time decisions in unpredictable emergencies that no written document could fully anticipate.

Your proxy is your voice when you don't have one. Choose that person carefully, and choose them for their judgment as well as their love for you.

2. The Two Documents You Need (And the Difference Between Them)

People often use "advance directive" as a catch-all term, which creates confusion. To be specific, there are two core documents that work together.

The first is a living will, a written statement of your medical preferences, covering things like resuscitation, mechanical ventilation, and artificial nutrition. The second is a durable power of attorney for health care, which is the legal document that officially names your health care proxy. According to the NIH National Institute on Aging, these two documents together form the foundation of ensuring your medical wishes are honored if you cannot communicate them yourself.

You need both. One tells doctors what you want, and the other names who speaks for you when you can't. Neither is a substitute for the other.

One more thing: requirements vary by state. A document valid in Florida may not be recognized in Arizona. If you split time between states or have family in different places, make sure your documents are compliant wherever you might receive care.

3. The Conversation Works Better Than Any Document

You can have perfectly drafted legal documents (witnessed, notarized, filed with your doctor) and they can still fail you, because in a real emergency those papers may not be immediately accessible. The hospital may not have them on file. Your proxy may be too shocked to locate them.

What consistently works is the conversation itself. When your proxy understands your values (why you'd say "no ventilator," under what circumstances, and what quality of life means to you), they can make the right call even without the paperwork in hand.

Have the conversation more than once. Have it when things are calm. Talk about specific scenarios. "If I had a massive stroke and couldn't recognize you anymore, I would want..." is a far more useful conversation than handing someone an envelope and saying "it's all in there."

A 2025 clinical trial published in JAMA Internal Medicine, one of the largest of its kind, involved nearly 65,000 older patients across 51 primary care practices. It found that structured advance care planning conversations in primary care settings nearly doubled the rate of documented end-of-life preferences compared to standard care (12% vs. 6.6%). Intentional, guided conversations move people to act.

4. Advance Directives Planning Needs Annual Reviews

It's a mistake to treat these documents like a will: drafted once, filed away, never touched again.

Your health changes. Your relationships change. The person you named as proxy ten years ago may have moved across the country, developed health problems of their own, or changed in ways that make them less suited to advocate for you under pressure. The NIH National Institute on Aging specifically recommends treating advance directives as living documents, reviewed at least once a year.

Think of it like a smoke detector: you don't install it once and assume it'll work forever. You check it and update it when needed.

Review your documents after any significant health event, such as a new diagnosis, a hospitalization, or a major surgery. Review them after big life changes too: a divorce, a death in the family, a move to a new state. And review them once a year regardless. Some people tie it to a birthday or a holiday, whatever makes it stick.

5. Choosing a Proxy for the Wrong Reasons

This might be the most important point. Most people choose their health care proxy based on proximity and love: their spouse, their oldest child, their closest sibling. That's understandable, but it's not always the right call.

The person you need as your proxy is someone who can hold your wishes above their own grief, who can walk into a hospital room, look a physician in the eye, and say "she wouldn't want this," even when the rest of the family is pushing back. That takes a particular kind of emotional steadiness that not everyone has, and it has nothing to do with how much someone loves you.

Ask yourself: Can this person handle conflict? Can they make decisions under pressure without needing consensus? Are they available, both geographically and in terms of their own health and schedule? Will they follow your wishes even if they disagree with them?

Have a frank conversation with the person you're considering before you name them. Don't assume. Make sure they're willing, they understand the responsibility, and they know what you'd want. Being someone's proxy is a real job.

Where to Start This Week

If you've been putting this off, there's a low-pressure way to begin. You don't need a lawyer to take the first step. Start by downloading your state's advance directive form. Most are free through your state health department or through nonprofits like CaringInfo. Then sit with it. Read through the questions. You don't have to fill it out in one sitting.

Next, have one honest conversation with your potential proxy. That alone puts you ahead of the majority of adults who have done nothing at all.

A Final Thought

The forms and the legal language matter, but what carries your wishes forward is the trust and understanding you build with the people closest to you. Do the paperwork. Then have the conversation, and have it again next year.
