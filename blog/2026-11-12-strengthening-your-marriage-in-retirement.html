Strengthening Your Marriage in Retirement: 5 Common Beliefs

November 12, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 6 min read

The belief you hear most often is that marriages get worse once both partners are home all day. Retirement brings more shared time, less structure and a changed routine, so it's easy to assume the marriage will strain under the pressure. Research on the marriage retirement relationship points in a different direction, and the evidence below can help you decide where to put your effort.

Belief 1: Most marriages get worse in later life

A study of trajectories of negative and positive marital quality during later life found that only about 12% of older adults experience a decline in marital quality. Where decline did occur, it was limited to positive quality, meaning less warmth, enjoyment and closeness. Negative quality, such as conflict and criticism, did not rise.

For most couples, then, the default is stability. For the minority who do drift, the loss is of good things rather than the arrival of bad ones, and that is useful to know. It means the work is mostly about keeping positive habits going, such as shared meals, laughter and time together, rather than putting out fires.

Belief 2: Your marriage has little effect on your mood

Some people treat marriage as background, something steady that doesn't need attention. Data suggest it shapes how you feel day to day. In a study of marital quality and depressive symptoms among older Hispanic adults, using Health and Retirement Study data on adults 51 and older, positive marital quality was associated with fewer depressive symptoms and negative marital quality with more.

The study describes an association, so it can't prove that a better marriage causes better mood. Still, the pattern held in both directions, and the authors suggest that improving marriage quality supports mental health. If one of you has felt low since retiring, the state of the relationship is worth looking at alongside sleep, activity and any conversation with your doctor.

Belief 3: Intimacy fades, and that's normal

Many couples assume physical closeness naturally ends and that it has no bearing on the relationship. A study of 620 long-term couples in the National Social Life, Health, and Aging Project (NSHAP), titled Sexual Expression and Subsequent Marital Quality Among Partnered Older Adults, linked couple-level patterns of sexual expression to later marital quality. Maintaining intimacy was associated with better satisfaction down the road.

Sexual expression covers more than intercourse. Touch, kissing, cuddling and affection count, and the study looked at patterns across the couple rather than one person's habits. If changes in health or medication have made things difficult, a doctor can discuss options, and the two of you can agree on what closeness looks like now.

Belief 4: Spending more time together will bring you closer

It seems logical that more hours at home lead to a stronger bond. The research above describes quality, not quantity: warmth, support and enjoyment, not simply proximity. Two people sharing a house all day without a plan can end up in parallel, each on a screen or a separate project.

Some structure helps. Many couples find it useful to keep a few separate routines, such as an individual walk, a hobby or a friend, and to schedule a few shared ones. A weekly meal you cook together or a regular walk gives positive time a place on the calendar. Belief 5: Strengthening a marriage in retirement takes big gestures

A second honeymoon or a major renovation is not what the evidence points toward. The findings above focus on positive marital quality and ongoing patterns of intimacy, which are built from small, repeated behaviors. Since decline in older marriages is mostly a fading of positive quality, small positive acts address the exact thing that tends to slip.

Options that fit this pattern include greeting each other warmly in the morning, saying thank you for routine tasks, sitting together in the evening without screens, and touching or holding hands during the day. None of these costs money, and each can start today.

A practical step: pick one positive habit and schedule it

Choose one habit from the list above and attach it to something you already do. For example, after your morning coffee, spend ten minutes talking before anyone opens a phone or turns on the news. Keep it for two weeks, then ask each other whether you'd like to keep it, change it or add another.

If you notice that conflict has grown or that either of you feels persistently sad, consider raising it with your doctor or a licensed couples counselor. Because the research links marital quality with mood, getting help for the relationship is a reasonable health step.

Common Questions

Do most marriages get worse after retirement?

No. A study of later-life marital trajectories found that only about 12% of older adults experience a decline in marital quality, and that decline was limited to positive quality, with no rise in negative quality. Most couples stay stable.

Does a better marriage help with depression in older adults?

Research using Health and Retirement Study data on adults 51 and older found that positive marital quality was associated with fewer depressive symptoms and negative quality with more. The study shows an association rather than proof of cause, but it suggests marriage quality supports mental health.

Does intimacy still matter for married couples over 60?

Yes. A study of 620 long-term couples found that patterns of sexual expression were linked to later marital quality. Intimacy includes affection and touch as well as sex, and a doctor can help if health problems are interfering.

What is one easy way to improve my marriage in retirement?

Pick one small positive habit, such as a daily ten-minute conversation or a weekly shared meal, and schedule it for two weeks. Small, repeated acts of warmth address the positive quality that is most likely to fade.
