Breast Screening Guidelines for Women 50: What's Changed

August 13, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read
Editorially reviewed by SteadiDay Health Editorial Team

These are the questions we hear most often about breast health screening. And honestly, it's no wonder — the guidelines shifted significantly in 2024, and a lot of women over 50 are unsure what applies to them now. Whether you've been getting regular mammograms for years or you've let a few slip by, here's what you actually need to know.

Did the breast screening guidelines for women 50 actually change?

Yes, and it's a bigger shift than most people realize. The US Preventive Services Task Force updated its recommendations in 2024, now advising that all women should begin biennial screening mammography at age 40, not 50. That's the headline number that got a lot of attention. But here's the part that matters specifically to you if you're in the 50–74 range: the every-other-year schedule remains the standard, and it carries a Grade B recommendation — meaning there's solid evidence it saves lives. If you've been going every two years since your 50s, you're on track. If you've been going annually, talk to your doctor about whether your personal risk profile changes that calculus. Women 75 and older currently sit in a gray zone — the USPSTF says evidence is insufficient to recommend routine screening, which doesn't mean stop, but it does mean that conversation with your doctor becomes more individual.

Why every other year — shouldn't more screening always be better?

It seems logical, but the evidence doesn't fully support it. Annual mammograms do catch some cancers earlier, but they also increase the chances of a false positive — a result that sends you back for more imaging or a biopsy that ultimately shows nothing worrying. Over ten years of annual screening, roughly 60% of women will have at least one false positive result. That's not a small number. For most women in the 50–74 range, biennial screening hits the right balance: meaningful cancer detection with fewer unnecessary follow-up procedures. That said, "most women" isn't everyone. If you have a strong family history, a BRCA gene variant, or prior abnormal results, annual screening may be exactly right for you. This is a conversation, not a one-size rule.

What does "dense breast tissue" mean for my screening plan?

Dense breasts aren't unusual — about 40% of women have them — but they do complicate mammography. Here's the problem: dense tissue and tumors both appear white on a mammogram, which makes it harder to spot cancer. Standard mammography is simply less sensitive when breast density is high. The 2024 American College of Radiology update on supplemental screening is direct about this: for average-risk women with dense breast tissue, supplemental MRI or abbreviated MRI is recommended alongside standard mammography. For women considered high-risk — based on genetics, family history, or prior radiation treatment to the chest — breast MRI is appropriate regardless of density. If you've never been told your breast density category, ask at your next mammogram. Radiologists are required to include it in your report, and many states now mandate that this information reaches you directly. Knowing your density isn't alarming — it's just useful.

What actually reduces my risk of dying from breast cancer?

Screening mammography reduces breast cancer mortality by approximately 30%, according to the ACR's 2024 screening criteria review. That's a substantial number. To put it plainly: catching breast cancer earlier, when it's smaller and hasn't spread, dramatically improves treatment outcomes. Beyond screening itself, there are lifestyle factors that genuinely move the needle. Maintaining a healthy weight after menopause matters — estrogen is produced in fat tissue, and elevated estrogen levels are linked to higher breast cancer risk. Limiting alcohol is real, not just general health advice; even one drink a day is associated with a modest increase in risk. Regular physical activity — we're talking 150 minutes of moderate movement per week — also appears protective. These aren't guarantees, but they're levers you actually control. I've skipped a few years of screening — is it too late to catch up?

It's not too late. But you're not alone in having gaps. A large national study found that among 6,814 screening-eligible women aged 50–74, only 76.4% adhered to USPSTF breast cancer screening guidelines — and adherence dropped sharply for women without insurance, without a primary care provider, or with lower household incomes. Life gets in the way. Costs, logistics, anxiety about results — all of it is real. The practical step right now is simple: schedule the mammogram. If cost is the barrier, federally funded programs like the CDC's National Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program offer free or low-cost screening for women who qualify. Most private insurance plans cover biennial mammograms at no out-of-pocket cost under the Affordable Care Act. Don't let a previous gap become a reason to keep waiting.

How do I know if I'm considered "high risk" for breast cancer?

High-risk status isn't always obvious, and a lot of women who qualify for more intensive screening don't know it. The main factors that push someone into the high-risk category include: a known BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene variant (or a first-degree relative who carries one), a lifetime breast cancer risk of 20% or greater based on risk-assessment models, a history of chest radiation therapy before age 30, or certain conditions like Li-Fraumeni or Cowden syndrome. If any of those apply to you, annual breast MRI alongside mammography is generally recommended — not instead of it. Unsure whether your family history is significant enough to flag? Ask your primary care doctor for a referral to a genetic counselor or a breast health specialist. They use standardized risk models that take your full picture into account. A 20-minute conversation can clarify years of uncertainty.

What should I actually do before my next mammogram appointment?

Show up prepared, and you'll get more out of the visit. Know your family history on both sides — maternal and paternal — and be specific about who had breast or ovarian cancer and at what age. Bring any prior mammogram records if you're seeing a new facility; radiologists compare images over time, and having your history matters. On the day itself, skip deodorant and antiperspirant — they can show up as artifacts on the image. Wear a two-piece outfit so you only need to undress from the waist up. Ask the technologist to tell you your breast density category if it's not already in your patient portal. And if you get a callback for additional imaging, try not to catastrophize — the majority of callbacks lead to a normal result after a closer look. Follow up promptly, ask questions, and bring someone with you if it helps you feel steadier. Breast screening guidelines for women 50 and older exist because this kind of follow-through genuinely saves lives. Showing up is the most important thing.

Common Questions

What are the current breast screening guidelines for women aged 50 to 74?

The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends biennial screening mammography — every two years — for women aged 40 to 74. For women in the 50–74 range, this schedule carries a Grade B recommendation, meaning there's strong evidence it reduces breast cancer mortality. Women with additional risk factors, like dense breast tissue or a family history of breast cancer, may benefit from a different schedule or supplemental imaging.

Should women over 75 still get mammograms?

The USPSTF currently says the evidence is insufficient to recommend routine screening for women 75 and older — not that screening should stop. For women in this age group, the decision should be made individually with a doctor, taking into account overall health, life expectancy, and personal preferences. Some women in their late 70s and 80s may benefit significantly from continued screening.

What extra screening do women with dense breasts need?

Women with dense breast tissue and average overall risk are advised to have supplemental MRI or abbreviated MRI alongside standard mammography, according to 2024 ACR guidelines. Dense tissue reduces mammography's ability to detect tumors. Your breast density is included in your mammogram report — ask your provider what category you fall into and whether additional imaging is right for you.

How can I get a mammogram if I don't have health insurance?

The CDC's National Breast and Cervical Cancer Early Detection Program provides free or low-cost mammograms for women who meet income and age requirements. Many community health centers and hospital systems also offer sliding-scale fees. Call 1-800-CDC-INFO or visit cdc.gov to find a program near you.
