How Your Diet Affects Biological Age After 60

September 21, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

You pour your morning coffee, maybe crack a couple of eggs, and breakfast feels like the last place you'd expect to make a dent in how fast you're aging. But a 2026 University of Sydney study in Aging Cell suggests what you eat can shift some of the measures researchers use to estimate how fast you're aging. Adults between 65 and 75 who were given meals lower in fat or lower in animal protein for four weeks showed changes in a combined score built from routine health measures such as cholesterol, blood sugar, blood pressure, and a marker of inflammation. The authors caution that this short-term change may reflect the body's quick response to food rather than slower aging. Because this research speaks directly to the 65–75 age window, the connection between diet and biological age after 60 feels a lot more personal than the usual general-population headlines.

What the Research Found

The University of Sydney trial randomly assigned 104 relatively healthy adults aged 65 to 75 to one of four diets that varied protein source (mostly animal versus semi-vegetarian) and the balance of fat and carbohydrate. All food was delivered to participants for four weeks. The researchers then used a method called the Klemera-Doubal Method, which combines several blood and health measures into one score, to estimate biological age. The high-fat omnivorous diet, the one closest to what participants usually ate, showed no meaningful change. Compared with that group, people on the omnivorous diet that was lower in fat and higher in carbohydrates had a significant drop in their score. The two semi-vegetarian groups showed similar drops, though not all of them were statistically significant. The researchers frame this as an early signal rather than a prescription, and say longer trials are needed to know whether it changes the risk of age-related disease.

Other research points the same way, though it can't show cause and effect. A 2025 study in npj Aging using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data looked at 2,260 US adults aged 50 and older at a single point in time. Higher saturated fat intake was linked to an older reading on one DNA-based aging test, while higher polyunsaturated fat and omega-3 intake were linked to younger readings on several others. A one-unit rise in the ratio of polyunsaturated to saturated fat, which is a large change in how you eat, was linked to about one year younger on a test called PhenoAge. The authors say randomized trials are needed to know whether changing your fats changes how you age.

Morning: Your First Real Decision of the Day

Back to that breakfast. If your usual morning involves eggs and bacon or a sausage scramble, you're not eating badly, but there's room to experiment. The University of Sydney findings pointed specifically to reductions in animal-based protein and dietary fat, which means shifting the ratio rather than eliminating them.

Try two eggs instead of three, and add half a cup of canned white beans on the side, sautéed with garlic in a teaspoon of olive oil. Beans are a legume, which puts them squarely in the category that's generating real research attention. In a 2026 crossover feeding trial in Clinical Nutrition ESPEN, 47 adults aged 60 and older ate two diets for eight weeks each, both following the Dietary Guidelines for Americans: one with lean pork and one with pulses (beans, lentils, and peas) as the main protein. Both diets improved blood sugar and most cholesterol-related markers and lowered total fat and visceral fat (the fat stored around the organs), with no difference between them. The pulse diet led to a larger drop in the share of body fat that was visceral. The authors concluded that the overall quality of the diet mattered more than the protein source.

Skip the butter on your toast and use a thin spread of almond butter instead. That small swap moves you from saturated fat toward unsaturated fat without changing the ritual of breakfast at all.

Midmorning: Where Habits Either Hold or Fall Apart

Around 10 or 10:30 is when a lot of people reach for something without thinking much about it. Cheese and crackers. A handful of mixed nuts (heavy on the cashews). A coffee with a splash of half-and-half that somehow became a generous pour.

None of those are catastrophic, but if you're trying to reduce dietary fat, the midmorning snack is worth a look. A small apple with a tablespoon of peanut butter, or a cup of plain Greek yogurt with some berries, keeps your blood sugar steady without loading up on saturated fat. If you use an app like SteadiDay, whose Premium plan connects directly to Apple Health, you can get a quick sense of how your morning has gone macronutrient-wise without obsessing over every gram. It's more like a gentle check-in than a surveillance system.

Lunch: The Easiest Place to Make the Biggest Shift

Lunch is where the research on diet and biological age after 60 becomes practical. This is the meal that most people have the most control over: you're usually home or packing your own, and there's no social pressure around the table.

A lentil soup with a slice of whole grain bread. A grain bowl with farro, roasted vegetables, chickpeas, and a lemon-tahini drizzle. A big salad with canned salmon (high in omega-3s, lower in saturated fat than most red meat), cucumber, olives, and avocado. These are ordinary meals rather than diet foods, and they happen to shift your macronutrient composition in the direction the research is pointing.

The University of Sydney study's strongest results came from the lower-fat, higher-carbohydrate omnivorous group, so the aim is a smaller proportion of animal foods rather than none at all. A salmon-and-vegetable lunch fits that framework exactly.

Afternoon: Movement and What You Eat Around It

If you take a walk in the afternoon, even a 20-minute one, the snacks around it are another easy place to put these ideas to work.

A small banana or a few whole grain crackers before a walk gives you steady energy without a spike. After, a handful of walnuts or a small portion of hummus with vegetables helps with recovery without pushing your saturated fat intake up. Walnuts are particularly worth mentioning, since they're one of the few whole foods with meaningful plant-based omega-3 content.

Weight loss may also play a part. A 2025 conference abstract from Wake Forest researchers in Innovation in Aging pooled 768 older adults (average age 67) from six calorie restriction trials. People assigned to cut calories lost about 18 pounds on average over six months, and they had larger drops in CRP (a marker of inflammation) and insulin than people who did not cut calories. Losing weight isn't right for every older adult, so talk to your doctor before cutting calories.

Dinner: Keeping It Simple Without Losing Satisfaction

Dinner tends to be where animal protein is most concentrated: a chicken breast, a piece of fish, ground beef in a pasta sauce. The research doesn't say to quit any of that; it points to reducing the proportion and paying attention to fat content.

A practical approach: make whatever protein you'd normally have, but use less of it and build the meal around vegetables and a whole grain or legume base. Half a chicken breast over a big bowl of roasted cauliflower, sweet potato, and quinoa feels like a complete meal, because it is one. You've reduced the animal protein load without the drama of "this is a diet dinner."

Fish twice a week fits the omega-3 data well. Salmon, sardines, mackerel, or even canned tuna (in water, not oil) all qualify. If you use olive oil for cooking, you're already choosing an unsaturated fat over saturated alternatives, which aligns with the PUFA-to-saturated fat ratio findings cited earlier.

The University of Sydney authors were clear that this research needs longer-term follow-up before anyone can make firm claims about disease prevention. What they found is a measurable short-term change in a combined health score, which may reflect the body's quick response to food rather than slower aging. That's enough to motivate a thoughtful experiment, though not enough to turn your entire diet upside down based on one study.

Common Questions

What does "biological age" mean, and how is it different from chronological age?

Chronological age is how many years you've been alive. Biological age is an estimate of how your body is functioning, built from measures like cholesterol, blood sugar, blood pressure, or chemical marks on DNA called epigenetic markers. Researchers still debate how best to measure it, and different methods can give different results. Two people who are both 68 years old can have meaningfully different biological ages depending on lifestyle, genetics, and diet.

Do adults over 65 need to worry about getting enough protein if they reduce animal-based sources?

This is a real concern, and it's worth discussing with your doctor or a registered dietitian. The University of Sydney study reduced animal-based protein but didn't eliminate it. Legumes, Greek yogurt, eggs, fish, and plant-based proteins like tofu can help maintain adequate intake. The PROT-AGE expert group recommends at least 1.0 to 1.2 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily for adults over 65, so intentional substitution matters more than simple reduction. The same group says people with severe kidney disease who are not on dialysis may need to limit protein, so ask your doctor if you have kidney disease.

How quickly might someone realistically expect to see changes in blood biomarkers from dietary adjustments?

The University of Sydney trial found changes in its combined score after four weeks, while all food was provided. The authors caution that short-term changes like these may reflect the body's quick response to food rather than a change in how fast you age. Individual results vary, and your doctor can order simple bloodwork to give you a baseline to compare against.

Is this research specific to adults 65 to 75, or does it apply to people in their 50s and early 60s too?

The University of Sydney study included only relatively healthy adults aged 65 to 75, and the authors note that this limits how far the results apply to other people. The NHANES study of dietary fats included adults 50 and older, but it shows links rather than cause and effect. Longer studies are needed to know whether these changes affect health over time at any age.
