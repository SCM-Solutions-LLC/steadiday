Does Your Tap Water Raise Your Dementia Risk?

June 15, 2026 • By SteadiDay Team • 7 min read

If you fill your glass from the kitchen tap each morning, you probably feel good about it. Maybe you ditched soda years ago, drink your eight glasses a day, and figure you're doing everything right. A sweeping new study, one that tracked more than 54,000 adults for nearly three decades, raises a question you may never have thought to ask: could the water itself be part of the problem?

According to the study, newly published in Alzheimer's & Dementia, the answer is possibly. The details are worth understanding, because along with raising a concern, this research points toward something useful you can do about it, starting at your next meal.

What the Study Found About Nitrate and Dementia Risk

Researchers from Edith Cowan University in Australia and the Danish Cancer Institute followed 54,000+ Danish adults for about 27 years. They recorded what people ate at the start of the study, estimated the nitrate in the tap water at their homes, and tracked who went on to develop dementia. What they found upended the usual conversation about nitrates: where your nitrate comes from may matter far more than how much of it you consume.

People who got the most nitrate from plant foods had about a 10% lower rate of dementia. People who got more from animal foods, processed meat, or tap water had a higher rate. That split (same molecule, opposite links) is the heart of this 27-year study on source-specific nitrate intake and dementia, and it's why the findings are generating so much attention in the scientific community. Because it was an observational study, the authors write in the full study report that it cannot prove cause and effect, and that the findings should be treated as a starting point for more research.

The drinking water finding is the newest part. The study linked nitrate in drinking water to dementia risk at levels far below legal limits. Compared with people whose home water had the least nitrate (an average of 0.8 milligrams per liter), people whose water averaged about 1.8 to 5.1 milligrams per liter had a 12% to 16% higher rate of dementia. The authors note that legal limits around the world range from about 44 to 50 milligrams of nitrate per liter. In the U.S., the EPA limit for nitrate is 10 milligrams per liter measured as nitrogen, which works out to about 44 milligrams per liter measured as nitrate.

Why Vegetables Get a Pass and Processed Meat Doesn't

Nitrate is nitrate, chemically speaking. Yet nitrate from vegetables was linked to lower dementia rates while nitrate from processed meat was linked to higher rates. Researchers think the difference lies in the company nitrate keeps.

When you eat vegetables, the nitrate arrives alongside a whole ecosystem of vitamins, antioxidants, and phytonutrients, particularly vitamin C and polyphenols. These compounds are thought to steer nitrate toward a beneficial conversion: your body turns it into nitric oxide, a molecule that relaxes and dilates blood vessels, improves circulation, and supports healthy blood flow to the brain. Good blood flow to the brain is, not surprisingly, closely tied to lower dementia risk. This explanation is still unproven: in the Danish study, the results did not clearly differ between people with higher and lower vitamin C intake.

Processed meat is a different story. The nitrates and nitrites in cured and preserved meats interact with proteins called amines in an environment that's low in protective antioxidants, producing compounds called nitrosamines instead. Nitrosamines have been associated with cellular damage, potentially including in brain tissue. A 2025 study in Neurology reinforced this picture, finding that higher processed red meat intake was linked to both a greater risk of dementia and measurably worse cognitive function, and that swapping one daily serving of processed red meat for nuts or legumes was associated with about a 19% lower dementia risk.

What surprised researchers about the water finding is that the mechanism is less clear. Tap water doesn't carry the protective antioxidants that vegetables do, but it also doesn't carry the amine-rich proteins of processed meat. Scientists suspect the nitrate in water may contribute to overall nitrate load in an unprotected form, arriving without the biological chaperones that make vegetable nitrate beneficial. It's a hypothesis that warrants a lot more research, and the study's authors are calling for that research.

The Numbers That Should Be on Your Radar

In concrete terms, lower dementia rates were seen at moderate to high intakes of plant nitrate, about 34 to 77 milligrams a day. The authors say that equals roughly half a cup to one and a half cups of raw leafy greens a day (in Denmark, much of it came from lettuce and potatoes). That amounts to a handful of greens at lunch, not a dramatic dietary overhaul.

On the water side, average levels of about 1.8 to 5.1 mg/L of nitrate were linked to higher dementia rates. If your U.S. water report lists nitrate "as nitrogen" (sometimes written nitrate-N), multiply that number by about 4.4 to compare it with the nitrate levels in this study. Most municipal water systems report their nitrate levels publicly; in the U.S., your utility is required to publish an annual Consumer Confidence Report. If you've never looked yours up, this study is a reasonable prompt to do so. For perspective, agricultural runoff and fertilizer use are the most common drivers of elevated nitrate in groundwater, which means the risk isn't evenly distributed geographically.

The researchers are not saying, "Stop drinking tap water." They write that further research and consideration of stricter water nitrate rules are needed, because the higher dementia rates showed up at levels well below current limits. They also note that other contaminants in water, such as aluminum and arsenic, could not be ruled out as part of the explanation.

What This Means for What You Eat

The dietary picture this study paints aligns closely with what a growing body of research has been building toward. A 2026 study in Neurology following a multiethnic cohort found that people who stuck most closely to a high-quality plant-based diet (one rich in whole grains, fruits, vegetables, and legumes) had a somewhat lower risk of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (about 7% lower). Notably, the researchers found that an unhealthful plant-based diet, heavy on refined grains and added sugars, was linked to higher risk. The quality of what you're eating matters as well as the category.

In practice, rather than obsessing over nitrate grams on a nutrition label, shift the balance of where your nitrate comes from. Leafy greens like spinach, arugula, and Swiss chard are among the richest natural sources of vegetable nitrate. Beets and beet juice have become popular partly for this reason. Adding more of these to your plate (as a side dish, blended into a smoothie, tucked into a grain bowl) moves you in the direction the evidence points.

Reducing processed meat is the other lever. Bacon, deli turkey, hot dogs and salami are the foods to treat as occasional rather than everyday. If you're a habitual sandwich-at-lunch person, swapping the deli meat for hummus and roasted vegetables a few days a week is an easy, low-friction change that the research supports.

What to Do About Your Drinking Water

The water piece is where many people feel a flicker of helplessness, and it's worth being practical here. First: check your local water quality report. In the U.S., you can find your utility's Consumer Confidence Report through EPA's Consumer Confidence Report page or search your city's name plus "water quality report." You won't know your nitrate level until you look.

If your levels are elevated, or if you want an extra layer of protection, the CDC lists reverse osmosis, distillation and ion exchange as ways to remove nitrate from drinking water. Standard carbon filters (the kind in a typical pitcher or faucet attachment) are not on that list, so check that any filter you buy is certified to reduce nitrate. Private wells are not covered by the EPA rules that protect public water systems, so the CDC advises testing well water at least once a year, including for nitrates.

Keep in mind that this is a single study, even if a very large and long one. The researchers themselves are careful to frame the water finding as a call for further investigation, not a public health emergency. It is a signal worth paying attention to, and one that regulatory agencies and scientists are now likely to follow up on in the years ahead.

The science of brain health is cumulative. The foods you eat most days, the water you drink most mornings, the small choices that accumulate over decades: those are what studies like this one are measuring. The choices this research points toward are modest ones: more greens, less processed meat, and a look at your water report. That's a manageable list.

If you pull up your city's water quality report, you may find your nitrate level is well below any threshold for concern. You may also notice you haven't eaten a green vegetable in days, which a bag of baby spinach in your next grocery order can fix. A study like this may not change everything, but it can nudge you toward the thing you already knew you should probably do.

Common Questions

Is nitrate in drinking water dangerous for brain health, even below the legal limit?

A 27-year study of more than 54,000 Danish adults published in Alzheimer's & Dementia found that people whose tap water averaged about 1.8 to 5.1 milligrams of nitrate per liter had a 12% to 16% higher rate of dementia than people with the lowest levels. That is far below legal limits, which range from about 44 to 50 mg/L of nitrate (the U.S. limit is 10 mg/L measured as nitrogen, about 44 mg/L as nitrate). The study was observational, so it cannot prove that nitrate caused the dementia, and the authors call for more research and consideration of stricter water nitrate rules.

If nitrate is bad for the brain, why do vegetables lower dementia risk?

Researchers think the vitamins, antioxidants, and other compounds in vegetables help guide nitrate toward a beneficial conversion into nitric oxide, a molecule that supports healthy blood flow to the brain. Nitrate from processed meat may instead form harmful compounds called nitrosamines. Why tap water nitrate was linked to higher risk is less clear, and these explanations are still being studied.

Do standard water filters remove nitrate from tap water?

Standard carbon filters, including most pitcher-style and faucet-mounted filters, are not among the treatments the CDC lists for removing nitrate. The CDC lists reverse osmosis, distillation, and ion exchange, and suggests choosing a system certified to remove nitrate. These are worth considering if your local water report shows elevated levels or if you're on a private well, which the CDC advises testing at least once a year.

How much vegetable nitrate do I need to eat to potentially lower my dementia risk?

The study linked moderate to high intakes of plant nitrate (about 34 to 77 milligrams a day) with lower dementia rates over nearly three decades of follow-up. The authors say that equals roughly half a cup to one and a half cups of raw leafy greens a day. Leafy greens like spinach, arugula, Swiss chard, and beets are among the richest natural sources of vegetable nitrate.
